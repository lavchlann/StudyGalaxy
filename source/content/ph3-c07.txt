## 7.1 | Magnetic flux
@p **Magnetic flux** measures how much field passes through a surface: $\Phi=BA\cos\theta$ in **webers (Wb)**, where $A$ is the area and $\theta$ is the angle between the field and the **normal** (perpendicular) to the surface. Flux is largest when the field is perpendicular to the loop's plane and zero when the field runs parallel to the plane.
@key $$\Phi=BA\cos\theta$$
@ex Flux
Q: A $0.020\ \text{m}^2$ coil is perpendicular to a $0.50\ \text{T}$ field. Find the flux, and the flux after turning $60^\circ$.
S: $\Phi=BA\cos0^\circ=0.50(0.020)=0.010\ \text{Wb}$.
S: At $60^\circ$: $\Phi=0.010\cos60^\circ=0.0050\ \text{Wb}$.
A: $0.010\ \text{Wb}$ and $0.0050\ \text{Wb}$.
@warn $\theta$ is measured from the **normal**, not from the plane of the coil. If a question gives the angle to the plane, subtract it from $90^\circ$.

## 7.2 | Faraday's and Lenz's laws
@p **Faraday's law:** the induced emf equals the rate of change of flux linkage: $\varepsilon=-N\dfrac{\Delta\Phi}{\Delta t}$. **Lenz's law** (the minus sign): the induced current's own field **opposes the change** in flux that caused it. This is conservation of energy: if induced fields helped the change, energy would appear from nothing.
@key $$\varepsilon=-N\frac{\Delta\Phi}{\Delta t}$$
@ex Changing flux
Q: A $200$-turn coil has flux falling from $0.010\ \text{Wb}$ to zero in $0.050\ \text{s}$. Find the average emf.
S: $|\varepsilon|=N\frac{\Delta\Phi}{\Delta t}=200\times\frac{0.010}{0.050}=40\ \text{V}$.
A: $40\ \text{V}$
@p **Using Lenz.** A north pole approaching a loop increases flux through it, so the loop develops a current whose field also points toward the approaching magnet (a north face) to repel it. A magnet pulled away is attracted. In both cases the induced current makes the motion **harder**.

## 7.3 | Moving conductors
@p A conductor of length $L$ moving at speed $v$ perpendicular to a field $B$ has emf $\varepsilon=BLv$. If connected to a circuit of resistance $R$ the current is $I=\frac{\varepsilon}{R}$, and the magnetic force on it is $BIL$, opposing the motion.
@ex Sliding rod
Q: A $0.50\ \text{m}$ rod moves at $4.0\ \text{m s}^{-1}$ perpendicular to a $0.30\ \text{T}$ field. Find the emf.
S: $\varepsilon=BLv=0.30(0.50)(4.0)=0.60\ \text{V}$.
A: $0.60\ \text{V}$
@p **Eddy currents.** A conductor moving through a changing field has circulating currents induced in it. Lenz's law means they oppose the motion, which is used for braking and cause heating in transformer cores (reduced by laminating the core).

## 7.4 | Generators and AC
@p In an **AC generator** a coil rotates in a magnetic field and the flux changes sinusoidally. The emf is **greatest when the coil's plane is parallel to the field** (the flux is zero but changing fastest) and **zero when the plane is perpendicular to the field** (maximum flux, zero rate of change). A split-ring commutator would give DC; slip rings give AC.
@p **RMS values.** For a sinusoidal supply $V_{\text{rms}}=\dfrac{V_{\text{peak}}}{\sqrt2}$ and $I_{\text{rms}}=\dfrac{I_{\text{peak}}}{\sqrt2}$. The RMS value is the equivalent steady value that would deliver the same power: $P=V_{\text{rms}}I_{\text{rms}}=\dfrac{V_{\text{rms}}^2}{R}$.
@ex RMS
Q: The mains supply has $V_{\text{rms}}=240\ \text{V}$. Find the peak voltage.
S: $V_{\text{peak}}=\sqrt2\times240=339\ \text{V}$.
A: $339\ \text{V}$
@warn Household "240 V" is an RMS value, so the voltage actually swings between about $-339\ \text{V}$ and $+339\ \text{V}$.

## X | Exam toolkit
@p **Explaining induction.** A complete answer has three parts: (1) the flux through the circuit is changing, (2) so an emf is induced (Faraday), (3) and the direction is such as to oppose the change (Lenz). Use the word **flux** and the word **change**.
@p **Graph questions.** Flux against time is a cosine for a rotating coil; the emf is the gradient of the flux graph (negative), which gives a sine curve.
