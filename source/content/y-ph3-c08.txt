## 8.1
@p **Why a transformer needs AC.** The primary current creates a magnetic flux in the iron core. Only a **changing** flux induces an emf in the secondary (Faraday's law). Alternating current changes direction constantly, so the flux keeps changing and an alternating emf appears in the secondary. With steady DC there is a brief pulse when switched on, then nothing.
@p **Turns ratio both ways.** For an ideal transformer $\frac{V_s}{V_p}=\frac{N_s}{N_p}$ and, because power in equals power out, $\frac{I_s}{I_p}=\frac{N_p}{N_s}$. A step-up transformer raises voltage and lowers current by the same factor. It does not create energy.
@ex Worded problem: phone charger
Q: A charger's transformer converts 240 V mains to 6.0 V. The primary has 2000 turns. How many secondary turns are needed, and what primary current flows when the output current is 1.2 A (ideal transformer)?
S: $N_s=N_p\frac{V_s}{V_p}=2000\times\frac{6.0}{240}=50$ turns.
S: $V_pI_p=V_sI_s\Rightarrow I_p=\frac{6.0\times1.2}{240}=0.030$ A.
A: 50 turns; $0.030$ A in the primary.
@fc def | Transformer voltage–turns equation. || $\dfrac{V_s}{V_p}=\dfrac{N_s}{N_p}$
@fc def | Ideal transformer power and current relationship. || $V_pI_p=V_sI_s$, so $\dfrac{I_s}{I_p}=\dfrac{N_p}{N_s}$.
@fc def | Why won't a transformer work on steady DC? || Steady current produces constant flux, and only changing flux induces an emf in the secondary.
@fc word | Step-up transformer with turns ratio 1:20 and 12 V input. Output voltage? || $12\times20=240$ V.
## 8.2
@p **Where the energy goes and how it is reduced.** Resistive heating in the copper windings ($I^2R$): use thick, low-resistance wire. Eddy currents in the core: use a **laminated** core of thin insulated iron sheets so the circulating currents are small. Hysteresis (repeated magnetising of the core): use soft iron, which magnetises and demagnetises easily. Flux leakage: wind the coils on top of each other on a closed core.
@ex Worded problem: real transformer
Q: A transformer is 92% efficient. Its primary draws 2.5 A from a 240 V supply. The output voltage is 24 V. Find the output current.
S: $P_{in}=240\times2.5=600$ W, so $P_{out}=0.92\times600=552$ W.
S: $I_s=\frac{552}{24}=23$ A.
A: $23$ A
@fc def | Four energy losses in a real transformer and how to reduce them. || Resistive heating (thick copper wire); eddy currents (laminated core); hysteresis (soft iron core); flux leakage (closely wound coils on a closed core).
@fc def | Why is a transformer core laminated? || Thin insulated layers break up eddy current paths, reducing heating losses in the core.
@fc def | Efficiency formula. || $\eta=\dfrac{P_{out}}{P_{in}}\times100\%$
## 8.3
@p **The whole grid in one picture.** Power stations generate at about 20 kV. Step-up transformers raise this to hundreds of kilovolts for long-distance lines, cutting the current and therefore the $I^2R$ loss. Step-down transformers at substations bring it down in stages to 240 V for homes. Remember that the voltage **drop** along the cable is $IR$, not the transmission voltage, so use $P_{loss}=I^2R$ with the line current.
@ex Worded problem: comparing transmission voltages
Q: A town needs 2.0 MW delivered through lines of total resistance 4.0 Ω. Compare the power lost if transmitted at 22 kV and at 220 kV.
S: At 22 kV: $I=\frac{2.0\times10^6}{22\times10^3}\approx91$ A, $P_{loss}=91^2\times4.0\approx3.3\times10^4$ W (about 1.7%).
S: At 220 kV: $I\approx9.1$ A, $P_{loss}\approx330$ W.
A: Ten times the voltage gives one-hundredth of the loss: about 33 kW versus 0.33 kW.
@fc def | Power lost in transmission lines. || $P_{loss}=I^2R$, where $I$ is the line current and $R$ the line resistance.
@fc word | Transmission voltage is increased by a factor of 10. Effect on line loss? || Current falls by 10, so $I^2R$ loss falls by a factor of 100.
@fc word | Why not use $P=\frac{V^2}{R}$ with the transmission voltage to find the line loss? || The transmission voltage is not across the cable. Only the small voltage drop $IR$ is, so use $I^2R$ with the line current.
