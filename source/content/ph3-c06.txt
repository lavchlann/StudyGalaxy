## 6.1 | Magnetic fields
@p Magnetic field lines leave the **north** pole and enter the **south** pole outside a magnet, and they never cross. A magnetic field is measured in **tesla (T)**. **Right-hand grip rule for a straight wire:** thumb along the conventional current, fingers curl in the direction of the field. **Solenoid:** curl fingers in the direction of the current in the coils and the thumb points to the **north** end; the field inside is nearly uniform.
@p The field around a long wire is $B=\dfrac{\mu_0I}{2\pi r}$ with $\mu_0=4\pi\times10^{-7}\ \text{T m A}^{-1}$, so it falls with distance. A solenoid's field increases with current and with the number of turns per metre.
@warn Conventional current flows from **positive to negative**, opposite to electron flow. Use conventional current for every right-hand rule unless a question tells you otherwise.

## 6.2 | Force on a current-carrying conductor
@p A wire carrying current $I$ in a perpendicular field $B$ experiences a force $F=BIL\sin\theta$, where $\theta$ is the angle between the current and the field. **Right-hand palm (or slap) rule:** fingers along the field, thumb along the conventional current, and the force comes out of the palm. The force is perpendicular to **both** the field and the current.
@key $$F=BIL\sin\theta$$
@ex Wire in a field
Q: A $0.30\ \text{m}$ wire carries $5.0\ \text{A}$ at right angles to a $0.40\ \text{T}$ field. Find the force.
S: $F=BIL=0.40(5.0)(0.30)=0.60\ \text{N}$.
A: $0.60\ \text{N}$
@p **Parallel wires.** Currents in the **same** direction attract; opposite directions repel. This is how the ampere is defined.

## 6.3 | Force on a moving charge
@p A charge $q$ moving at speed $v$ at angle $\theta$ to a magnetic field feels $F=qvB\sin\theta$. The force is **perpendicular to the velocity**, so it does **no work** and changes direction, not speed. For a negative charge reverse the direction from the right-hand rule.
@key $$F=qvB\sin\theta,\qquad r=\frac{mv}{qB},\qquad T=\frac{2\pi m}{qB}$$
@ex Path of a proton
Q: A proton moves at $2.0\times10^6\ \text{m s}^{-1}$ perpendicular to a $0.50\ \text{T}$ field. Find the radius of its circular path.
S: $\frac{mv^2}{r}=qvB\Rightarrow r=\frac{mv}{qB}=\frac{(1.67\times10^{-27})(2.0\times10^6)}{(1.60\times10^{-19})(0.50)}=4.2\times10^{-2}\ \text{m}$.
A: $4.2\ \text{cm}$
@int lorentz | Change the speed and the field strength and see the circular path of a proton.
@p **Velocity selector.** With electric and magnetic fields perpendicular to each other and to the velocity, a charge passes undeflected when $qE=qvB$, so $v=\dfrac EB$.
@warn The magnetic force does **no work** on a moving charge. It cannot speed a particle up; only an electric field can.

## 6.4 | Motors and applications
@p In a **DC motor** a current-carrying coil sits in a magnetic field. The forces on opposite sides of the coil are in opposite directions, producing a **torque**. A **split-ring commutator** reverses the current every half turn so the coil keeps turning in the same direction. Increasing the current, the field strength, the number of turns or the coil area increases the torque.
@p **Mass spectrometer.** Ions are accelerated through a known potential, then enter a magnetic field. They move in circles of radius $r=\frac{mv}{qB}$, so heavier ions travel on larger circles and are separated by mass.
