## 3.4
@p **What this topic is about.** The quotient rule differentiates one function divided by another, such as $\frac{x}{x^2+1}$, $\frac{e^{2x}}{x}$ or $\frac{\ln x}{x}$. Quotients model concentrations, averages and efficiencies, and they appear in stationary-point and tangent questions.
@key $$\frac{d}{dx}\left(\frac uv\right)=\frac{u'v-uv'}{v^2}$$ Memory aid: "low d-high minus high d-low, over low squared", where high $=u$ (top) and low $=v$ (bottom).
@p **Order matters.** Unlike the product rule, the quotient rule has a **minus** sign, so swapping the two terms in the numerator flips the sign of your answer. Check your order with a simple case: $\frac{x}{1}$ should differentiate to 1; with $u=x,v=1$: $\frac{1\cdot1-x\cdot0}{1}=1$ ✓.
@p **Quotient or product?** Any quotient can be written as a product with a negative power: $\frac{u}{v}=uv^{-1}$. If the bottom is a single power of $x$ (like $x^3$) it is often quicker to rewrite and use the product rule or even just the power rule. If the bottom is a sum ($x^2+1$), the quotient rule is usually cleaner.
@p **Simplifying the result.** Expand the numerator carefully with brackets, collect like terms, and factorise if possible. Usually leave the denominator as $v^2$ (do not expand it). Then common factors between top and bottom may cancel.
@ex Quotient with a polynomial bottom (full working)
Q: Differentiate $y=\dfrac{x}{x^2+1}$ and find the stationary points.
S: **Set up:** $u=x$, $u'=1$; $v=x^2+1$, $v'=2x$.
S: **Apply the rule:** $y'=\dfrac{1\cdot(x^2+1)-x\cdot2x}{(x^2+1)^2}$.
S: **Simplify the numerator:** $x^2+1-2x^2=1-x^2$. So $y'=\dfrac{1-x^2}{(x^2+1)^2}$.
S: **Stationary points:** a fraction is zero when its numerator is zero: $1-x^2=0\Rightarrow x=\pm1$.
S: **$y$-values:** $y(1)=\frac12$, $y(-1)=-\frac12$.
S: **Nature:** $y'$ is positive between $-1$ and $1$ and negative outside, so $\left(1,\frac12\right)$ is a maximum and $\left(-1,-\frac12\right)$ a minimum.
A: $y'=\dfrac{1-x^2}{(x^2+1)^2}$; max $\left(1,\frac12\right)$, min $\left(-1,-\frac12\right)$
@ex Quotient with an exponential (full working)
Q: Differentiate $y=\dfrac{e^{2x}}{x}$.
S: **Set up:** $u=e^{2x}$, $u'=2e^{2x}$; $v=x$, $v'=1$.
S: **Apply the rule:** $y'=\dfrac{2e^{2x}\cdot x-e^{2x}\cdot1}{x^2}$.
S: **Factorise the numerator:** $e^{2x}(2x-1)$.
S: **Final form:** $y'=\dfrac{e^{2x}(2x-1)}{x^2}$.
S: **Note:** this is zero only when $2x-1=0$, i.e. $x=\frac12$, because $e^{2x}>0$.
A: $\dfrac{e^{2x}(2x-1)}{x^2}$
@ex Quotient with a log (full working)
Q: Find the maximum value of $y=\dfrac{\ln x}{x}$ for $x>0$.
S: **Set up:** $u=\ln x$, $u'=\frac1x$; $v=x$, $v'=1$.
S: **Apply the rule:** $y'=\dfrac{\frac1x\cdot x-\ln x\cdot1}{x^2}=\dfrac{1-\ln x}{x^2}$.
S: **Set to zero:** $1-\ln x=0\Rightarrow x=e$.
S: **Nature:** for $x<e$, $\ln x<1$ so $y'>0$; for $x>e$, $y'<0$. Increasing then decreasing: a maximum.
S: **Maximum value:** $y(e)=\dfrac{\ln e}{e}=\dfrac1e$.
A: Maximum value $\frac{1}{e}$ at $x=e$
@ex Quotient with trig, simplified with an identity (full working)
Q: Differentiate $y=\dfrac{\cos x}{1+\sin x}$ and simplify.
S: **Set up:** $u=\cos x$, $u'=-\sin x$; $v=1+\sin x$, $v'=\cos x$.
S: **Apply the rule:** $y'=\dfrac{-\sin x(1+\sin x)-\cos x\cdot\cos x}{(1+\sin x)^2}$.
S: **Expand the numerator:** $-\sin x-\sin^2x-\cos^2x$.
S: **Use $\sin^2x+\cos^2x=1$:** numerator $=-\sin x-1=-(1+\sin x)$.
S: **Cancel one factor of $(1+\sin x)$:** $y'=\dfrac{-(1+\sin x)}{(1+\sin x)^2}=-\dfrac1{1+\sin x}$.
A: $-\dfrac{1}{1+\sin x}$
@warn Writing $\frac{uv'-u'v}{v^2}$ (wrong order) gives the negative of the correct answer. Also do not cancel terms across a minus sign in the numerator before simplifying properly.
@p **Exam tip.** If a question says "hence find the stationary points", only the numerator matters: set it equal to zero and remember to exclude values that make the denominator zero.

A: 3/(x+3)^2
AD: $\dfrac{3}{(x+3)^2}$
SOL: $u=x$, $u'=1$; $v=x+3$, $v'=1$. || $y'=\frac{1(x+3)-x(1)}{(x+3)^2}$. || Numerator: $x+3-x=3$. || So $y'=\frac{3}{(x+3)^2}$.
H: Low d-high minus high d-low. || Both derivatives are 1. || Simplify the numerator.
@check Quotient with e | Differentiate $y=\dfrac{e^{x}}{x^2}$.

ALT: (e^x(x-2))/x^3 || e^x(x-2)/x^3
AD: $\dfrac{e^x(x-2)}{x^3}$

@check Quotient with trig | Differentiate $y=\dfrac{\sin x}{x}$.

ALT: (x cos x - sin x)/x^2 || (xcos(x)-sin(x))/x^2

@check Stationary point | Find the $x$-coordinate of the stationary point of $y=\dfrac{\ln x}{x^2}$.
A: sqrte
ALT: e^(1/2) || √e || sqrt(e)
AD: $x=\sqrt e$

@check Value of derivative | Find $f'(0)$ for $f(x)=\dfrac{x+1}{x^2+1}$.

## 3.5
@p **What this topic is about.** Using the rules to answer applied questions: tangents and normals, rates of change in context, kinematics (displacement, velocity, acceleration) and simple optimisation. This is where derivatives earn most of their marks in the exam.
@key **Tangent at $x=a$:** $y-f(a)=f'(a)(x-a)$. **Normal at $x=a$:** gradient $-\frac{1}{f'(a)}$ (perpendicular gradients multiply to $-1$). If $f'(a)=0$ the tangent is horizontal and the normal is the vertical line $x=a$.
@key **Kinematics:** displacement $x(t)$; velocity $v=\frac{dx}{dt}$; acceleration $a=\frac{dv}{dt}$. "At rest" means $v=0$. "Initially" means $t=0$. Speed is $|v|$. The particle moves in the positive direction when $v>0$.
@p **Tangent and normal routine.** (1) Find the point $(a,f(a))$. (2) Differentiate. (3) Substitute to get the gradient $m=f'(a)$. (4) Tangent: $y-y_1=m(x-x_1)$. (5) Normal: use $-\frac1m$. (6) Rearrange to the form asked (usually $y=mx+c$ or $ax+by+c=0$). Give exact values.
@p **Rates of change in context.** "How fast", "rate of change", "rate of increase" all mean the derivative with respect to time (or the stated variable). Always give units: if $V$ is in litres and $t$ in minutes, $\frac{dV}{dt}$ is in litres per minute. A negative rate means decreasing.
@ex Tangent and normal (full working)
Q: Find the equations of the tangent and normal to $y=xe^{x}$ at $x=0$.
S: **Point:** $y=0\cdot e^0=0$, so $(0,0)$.
S: **Derivative (product rule):** $y'=e^x+xe^x=e^x(1+x)$.
S: **Gradient:** at $x=0$, $m=e^0(1)=1$.
S: **Tangent:** $y-0=1(x-0)\Rightarrow y=x$.
S: **Normal gradient:** $-\frac1m=-1$, so the normal is $y=-x$.
A: Tangent $y=x$; normal $y=-x$
@ex Kinematics (full working)
Q: A particle moves with displacement $x=te^{-0.5t}$ m for $t\ge0$ s. Find when it is at rest, its displacement then, and its acceleration at that time.
S: **Velocity (product rule):** $u=t$, $u'=1$; $v=e^{-0.5t}$, $v'=-0.5e^{-0.5t}$. So $v=e^{-0.5t}-0.5te^{-0.5t}=e^{-0.5t}(1-0.5t)$.
S: **At rest:** $v=0\Rightarrow1-0.5t=0\Rightarrow t=2$ s.
S: **Displacement then:** $x(2)=2e^{-1}\approx0.736$ m.
S: **Acceleration:** differentiate $v=e^{-0.5t}(1-0.5t)$ with the product rule: $a=-0.5e^{-0.5t}(1-0.5t)+e^{-0.5t}(-0.5)=e^{-0.5t}(0.25t-1)$.
S: **At $t=2$:** $a=e^{-1}(0.5-1)=-0.5e^{-1}\approx-0.184$ m/s$^2$.
A: At rest at $t=2$ s, $x\approx0.736$ m, $a\approx-0.18$ m/s²
@ex Rate of change with a quotient (full working)
Q: A drug's concentration is $C=\dfrac{20t}{t^2+4}$ mg/L after $t$ hours. Find when the concentration is greatest and its value.
S: **Quotient rule:** $u=20t$, $u'=20$; $v=t^2+4$, $v'=2t$.
S: **Derivative:** $C'=\dfrac{20(t^2+4)-20t\cdot2t}{(t^2+4)^2}=\dfrac{80-20t^2}{(t^2+4)^2}=\dfrac{20(4-t^2)}{(t^2+4)^2}$.
S: **Set to zero:** $4-t^2=0\Rightarrow t=2$ (time is positive).
S: **Check it is a maximum:** for $0<t<2$, $C'>0$ (rising); for $t>2$, $C'<0$ (falling).
S: **Value:** $C(2)=\dfrac{40}{8}=5$ mg/L.
A: Greatest after 2 hours, at 5 mg/L
@ex Simple optimisation (full working)
Q: A rectangle has perimeter 40 cm. Find the dimensions that give the largest area.
S: **Variables:** let the width be $x$; then the length is $20-x$ (half the perimeter is 20).
S: **Area function:** $A=x(20-x)=20x-x^2$, with $0<x<20$.
S: **Differentiate:** $A'=20-2x$.
S: **Set to zero:** $x=10$.
S: **Confirm a maximum:** $A''=-2<0$.
S: **Answer:** $10$ cm by $10$ cm (a square), area $100$ cm$^2$.
A: $10\times10$ cm, area $100$ cm²
@warn The normal's gradient is the **negative reciprocal**, not just the negative. And for kinematics, "at rest" is $v=0$, not $x=0$.
@p **Exam tip.** Write a concluding sentence with units for every applied answer, e.g. "The particle is at rest after 2 seconds, 0.74 m from the origin".

## X
@p **Spotting the rule.** Sum or difference: differentiate term by term. Product of two functions of $x$: product rule. Fraction with $x$ in the bottom: quotient rule (or rewrite as a product). Function inside a function: chain rule. Many exam questions need two rules at once, e.g. a product whose second factor needs the chain rule.
@p **A verification habit.** After differentiating, test a value. For $y=xe^{-2x}$ at $x=0$: your $y'=e^{-2x}(1-2x)$ gives 1, and the graph visibly rises through the origin with gradient about 1. A ten-second check catches sign errors.
@ex Exam-style: rules combined (full working)
Q: Differentiate $y=\dfrac{xe^{x}}{x+1}$ and give the answer as a single fraction.
S: **Quotient rule with $u=xe^x$, $v=x+1$.** First find $u'$ with the product rule: $u'=e^x+xe^x=(x+1)e^x$. And $v'=1$.
S: **Apply:** $y'=\dfrac{(x+1)e^x(x+1)-xe^x\cdot1}{(x+1)^2}$.
S: **Factor $e^x$ from the numerator:** $e^x\left[(x+1)^2-x\right]$.
S: **Expand:** $(x+1)^2-x=x^2+2x+1-x=x^2+x+1$.
S: **Result:** $y'=\dfrac{e^x(x^2+x+1)}{(x+1)^2}$.
A: $\dfrac{e^x(x^2+x+1)}{(x+1)^2}$
@ex Exam-style: chain inside product (full working)
Q: Differentiate $y=x\sqrt{1-x^2}$ and find where the gradient is zero.
S: **Product rule:** $u=x$, $u'=1$; $v=(1-x^2)^{1/2}$, $v'=\frac12(1-x^2)^{-1/2}(-2x)=\dfrac{-x}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}$.
S: **Assemble:** $y'=\sqrt{1-x^2}+x\cdot\dfrac{-x}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}$.
S: **Common denominator:** $y'=\dfrac{(1-x^2)-x^2}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}=\dfrac{1-2x^2}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}$.
S: **Set the numerator to zero:** $1-2x^2=0\Rightarrow x=\pm\dfrac{1}{\sqrt2}$.
A: $y'=\dfrac{1-2x^2}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}$; zero at $x=\pm\frac{1}{\sqrt2}$
@warn Leave the denominator of a quotient-rule answer factorised, and do not forget to apply the chain rule inside a product or quotient.
