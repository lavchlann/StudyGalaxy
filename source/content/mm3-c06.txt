## 6.2 | Discrete random variables
@p A **random variable** $X$ assigns a number to each outcome of a random process. It is **discrete** if it takes separate values, like the number of heads in 3 tosses. Its **probability distribution** lists each value $x$ with $P(X=x)$, usually as a table or a rule.
@key Every valid distribution satisfies two conditions: $0\le P(X=x)\le1$ for each $x$, and $\sum P(X=x)=1$.
@ex Finding an unknown probability
Q: $X$ has $P(X=x)=kx$ for $x=1,2,3,4$. Find $k$ and $P(X\ge3)$.
S: $k(1+2+3+4)=1\Rightarrow k=\frac1{10}$.
S: $P(X\ge3)=\frac3{10}+\frac4{10}=\frac7{10}$.
A: $k=\frac1{10}$, $P(X\ge3)=\frac7{10}$
@p The **cumulative** probability $P(X\le x)$ adds up the values to the left. Complement rule: $P(X\ge x)=1-P(X<x)$.
@warn A distribution must sum to exactly 1. If your fractions do not, recheck the arithmetic before moving on, because every later answer depends on it.

## 6.3 | Expected value
@key $$E(X)=\mu=\sum x\,P(X=x)$$
@p The **expected value** is the long-run average, not necessarily a value $X$ can take. For a function: $E(aX+b)=aE(X)+b$.
@ex Expected value
Q: $X$: $0,1,2$ with $P=0.2,0.5,0.3$. Find $E(X)$ and $E(3X+1)$.
S: $E(X)=0(0.2)+1(0.5)+2(0.3)=1.1$.
S: $E(3X+1)=3(1.1)+1=4.3$.
A: $1.1$ and $4.3$
@ex Fair game
Q: You pay \$2 to roll a die and win \$6 for a six, else nothing. Is it fair?
S: Net gain $G$: $+4$ with prob $\frac16$; $-2$ with prob $\frac56$.
S: $E(G)=\frac46-\frac{10}6=-1$, so you lose \$1 on average. Not fair.
A: Not fair; expected loss \$1 per game.
@warn A **fair game** has $E(\text{net gain})=0$. Remember to subtract the cost to play.

## 6.4 | Variance and standard deviation
@key $$\operatorname{Var}(X)=E\big[(X-\mu)^2\big]=E(X^2)-\mu^2,\qquad\sigma=\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(X)}$$
@p Here $E(X^2)=\sum x^2P(X=x)$. For linear transformations: $\operatorname{Var}(aX+b)=a^2\operatorname{Var}(X)$ and $\sigma(aX+b)=|a|\sigma$. Adding a constant shifts the mean but does not change the spread.
@ex Variance
Q: Find $\operatorname{Var}(X)$ for the distribution above ($0,1,2$ with $0.2,0.5,0.3$).
S: $E(X^2)=0+1(0.5)+4(0.3)=1.7$.
S: $\operatorname{Var}=1.7-1.1^2=1.7-1.21=0.49$, so $\sigma=0.7$.
A: $\operatorname{Var}(X)=0.49$, $\sigma=0.7$
@warn $E(X^2)\ne\big(E(X)\big)^2$. The difference between them is exactly the variance.

## 6.5 | Modelling with discrete random variables
@p Build a model by listing outcomes, assigning probabilities, and checking they sum to 1. Use $E(X)$ to compare decisions, and $\sigma$ to compare risk: same mean, higher $\sigma$ means a riskier option.
@ex Comparing options
Q: Game A pays \$1 or \$9 equally likely; game B pays \$4 or \$6 equally likely. Compare.
S: Both have $E=5$.
S: Game A has $\operatorname{Var}=\frac12(1+81)-25=16$; game B $\operatorname{Var}=\frac12(16+36)-25=1$.
A: Same average, but A is much riskier ($\sigma=4$ vs $\sigma=1$).
