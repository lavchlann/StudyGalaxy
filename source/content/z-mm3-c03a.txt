## 3.2
@p **What this topic is about.** The chain rule differentiates a **function of a function** (a composite), such as $(3x^2+1)^5$, $\sqrt{2x-1}$, $e^{\sin x}$ or $\ln(\cos x)$. It is the single most-used rule in calculus: almost every exam derivative uses it somewhere, often combined with the product or quotient rule.
@key $$\text{If } y=f(u) \text{ and } u=g(x),\quad\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{dy}{du}\times\frac{du}{dx}\qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad\big[f(g(x))\big]'=f'\big(g(x)\big)\,g'(x)$$
@p **In words.** Differentiate the outside function, leaving the inside exactly as it is, then multiply by the derivative of the inside. Say it out loud while you work: "derivative of the outside, inside left alone, times derivative of the inside".
@p **How to identify the inside and outside.** Imagine calculating the value for a particular $x$. The operation you do **last** is the outside. For $\sqrt{1+e^{2x}}$ you would calculate $1+e^{2x}$ first and take the square root last, so the outside is the square root and the inside is $1+e^{2x}$. For $\sin^3x=(\sin x)^3$ you find $\sin x$ first and cube it last, so the outside is the cube.
@p **Why it works.** Rates multiply. If $y$ changes 5 times as fast as $u$, and $u$ changes 3 times as fast as $x$, then $y$ changes $5\times3=15$ times as fast as $x$. Leibniz notation makes this look like fractions cancelling: $\frac{dy}{du}\cdot\frac{du}{dx}=\frac{dy}{dx}$.
@key **Common special cases:** $\frac{d}{dx}(ax+b)^n=na(ax+b)^{n-1}$; $\frac{d}{dx}\big(f(x)\big)^n=n\big(f(x)\big)^{n-1}f'(x)$; $\frac{d}{dx}\sqrt{f(x)}=\frac{f'(x)}{2\sqrt{f(x)}}$; $\frac{d}{dx}e^{f(x)}=f'(x)e^{f(x)}$; $\frac{d}{dx}\ln f(x)=\frac{f'(x)}{f(x)}$; $\frac{d}{dx}\sin f(x)=f'(x)\cos f(x)$.
@ex Chain rule with a power (full working)
Q: Differentiate $y=(3x^2+1)^5$.
S: **Identify the parts:** outside is $(\ \ )^5$; inside is $u=3x^2+1$.
S: **Rewrite:** $y=u^5$.
S: **Differentiate each part:** $\dfrac{dy}{du}=5u^4$ and $\dfrac{du}{dx}=6x$.
S: **Multiply:** $\dfrac{dy}{dx}=5u^4\times6x=30xu^4$.
S: **Substitute back for $u$:** $\dfrac{dy}{dx}=30x(3x^2+1)^4$.
A: $30x(3x^2+1)^4$
@ex Chain rule with a root (full working)
Q: Differentiate $y=\sqrt{2x-1}$ and find the gradient at $x=5$.
S: **Write the root as a power:** $y=(2x-1)^{1/2}$.
S: **Outside:** bring the power down and reduce it by 1: $\frac12(2x-1)^{-1/2}$.
S: **Inside derivative:** $\frac{d}{dx}(2x-1)=2$.
S: **Multiply:** $\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac12(2x-1)^{-1/2}\times2=(2x-1)^{-1/2}=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2x-1}}$.
S: **Gradient at $x=5$:** $\dfrac1{\sqrt{9}}=\dfrac13$.
A: $\dfrac{dy}{dx}=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2x-1}}$; gradient $\frac13$ at $x=5$
@ex Chain rule applied twice (full working)
Q: Differentiate $y=\sqrt{1+e^{2x}}$.
S: **Outside layer:** square root, so write $y=(1+e^{2x})^{1/2}$ and differentiate the outside: $\frac12(1+e^{2x})^{-1/2}$.
S: **Middle layer:** the inside is $1+e^{2x}$; its derivative needs the chain rule again: $\frac{d}{dx}e^{2x}=2e^{2x}$.
S: **Multiply:** $\dfrac{dy}{dx}=\dfrac12(1+e^{2x})^{-1/2}\times2e^{2x}$.
S: **Simplify:** the $\frac12$ and 2 cancel: $\dfrac{dy}{dx}=\dfrac{e^{2x}}{\sqrt{1+e^{2x}}}$.
A: $\dfrac{e^{2x}}{\sqrt{1+e^{2x}}}$
@ex Powers of trig functions (full working)
Q: Differentiate $y=\sin^3(2x)$.
S: **Rewrite to see the layers:** $y=\big(\sin(2x)\big)^3$. There are three layers: cube, sine, and $2x$.
S: **Outside (cube):** $3\big(\sin(2x)\big)^2$.
S: **Middle (sine):** $\frac{d}{dx}\sin(2x)=\cos(2x)\times2$ (with the derivative of $2x$).
S: **Multiply all factors:** $3\sin^2(2x)\times2\cos(2x)$.
S: **Simplify:** $6\sin^2(2x)\cos(2x)$.
A: $6\sin^2(2x)\cos(2x)$
@ex Worded problem: related rates (full working)
Q: A circular oil spill has radius $r=2\sqrt t$ m after $t$ minutes. Use the chain rule to find how fast its area is growing at $t=4$.
S: **Write the area in terms of $r$:** $A=\pi r^2$, so $\dfrac{dA}{dr}=2\pi r$.
S: **Rate of change of $r$:** $r=2t^{1/2}\Rightarrow\dfrac{dr}{dt}=t^{-1/2}=\dfrac1{\sqrt t}$.
S: **Chain rule:** $\dfrac{dA}{dt}=\dfrac{dA}{dr}\times\dfrac{dr}{dt}=2\pi r\times\dfrac{1}{\sqrt t}$.
S: **At $t=4$:** $r=2\sqrt4=4$, so $\dfrac{dA}{dt}=2\pi(4)\times\dfrac12=4\pi$.
S: **Interpret:** the area is growing at $4\pi\approx12.6$ m$^2$ per minute.
A: $4\pi\approx12.6$ m² per minute
@warn The number-one chain rule error is forgetting to multiply by the derivative of the inside. After every derivative ask "did I multiply by $u'$?". Second most common: changing the inside, e.g. writing $5(6x)^4$ instead of $5(3x^2+1)^4$.
@p **Exam tip.** For "show that" questions, write the substitution $u=\ldots$, $\frac{du}{dx}=\ldots$, $\frac{dy}{du}=\ldots$ on separate lines. It makes your method visible even if an arithmetic slip happens later.
@check Power chain | Differentiate $y=(2x^3-5)^4$.
A: 24x^2(2x^3-5)^3
ALT: 24x²(2x³-5)³
AD: $24x^2(2x^3-5)^3$
SOL: Let $u=2x^3-5$, so $y=u^4$. || $\frac{dy}{du}=4u^3$ and $\frac{du}{dx}=6x^2$. || Multiply: $4u^3\times6x^2=24x^2u^3$. || Substitute back: $\frac{dy}{dx}=24x^2(2x^3-5)^3$.
H: Identify the inside. || Differentiate $u^4$ and the inside separately. || Multiply and substitute back.
@check Root chain | Differentiate $y=\sqrt{x^2+16}$.
A: x/sqrt(x^2+16)
ALT: x/(x^2+16)^(1/2) || x/√(x^2+16)
AD: $\dfrac{x}{\sqrt{x^2+16}}$
SOL: Write $y=(x^2+16)^{1/2}$. || Outside derivative: $\frac12(x^2+16)^{-1/2}$. || Inside derivative: $2x$. || Multiply: $\frac12\times2x\,(x^2+16)^{-1/2}=\frac{x}{\sqrt{x^2+16}}$.
H: Write the root as a power $\frac12$. || The inside derivative is $2x$. || Simplify $\frac12\times2x$.
@check Exponential chain | Differentiate $y=e^{\sin x}$.
A: cosxe^(sinx)
ALT: cos(x)e^(sin(x)) || e^(sinx)cosx || cosx e^sinx
AD: $\cos x\,e^{\sin x}$
SOL: The outside is $e^{(\ )}$, which differentiates to itself. || The inside is $\sin x$, whose derivative is $\cos x$. || Multiply: $e^{\sin x}\times\cos x$. || So $\frac{dy}{dx}=\cos x\,e^{\sin x}$.
H: The outside is the exponential. || Inside derivative of $\sin x$. || Multiply.
@check Gradient | Find the gradient of $y=(x^2-3)^3$ at $x=2$.
A: 12
ALT: m=12
AD: $12$
SOL: Chain rule: $\frac{dy}{dx}=3(x^2-3)^2\times2x=6x(x^2-3)^2$. || Substitute $x=2$: $6(2)(4-3)^2$. || $=12\times1$. || The gradient is 12.
H: Differentiate with the chain rule. || Substitute $x=2$. || $(4-3)^2=1$.
@check Three layers | Differentiate $y=\cos^2(3x)$.
A: -6sin(3x)cos(3x)
ALT: -6cos(3x)sin(3x) || -3sin(6x)
AD: $-6\sin(3x)\cos(3x)$ (or $-3\sin6x$)
SOL: Rewrite as $y=\big(\cos(3x)\big)^2$. || Outside: $2\cos(3x)$. || Inside: $\frac{d}{dx}\cos(3x)=-3\sin(3x)$. || Multiply: $2\cos(3x)\times(-3\sin(3x))=-6\sin(3x)\cos(3x)$, which also equals $-3\sin(6x)$.
H: $\cos^2(3x)=(\cos3x)^2$. || Differentiate the square first. || Then $\cos(3x)$ with its chain factor 3.
## 3.3
@p **What this topic is about.** The product rule differentiates two functions of $x$ multiplied together, such as $x^2e^{3x}$, $x\ln x$ or $x\sin x$. Products are everywhere in modelling: a damped oscillation $e^{-t}\sin t$, or a drug model $te^{-t}$.
@key $$\frac{d}{dx}(uv)=u'v+uv'\qquad\text{(derivative of the first times the second, plus the first times the derivative of the second)}$$
@p **Why not just multiply the derivatives?** Try $y=x\cdot x=x^2$. The true derivative is $2x$. Multiplying the derivatives would give $1\times1=1$, which is wrong. The product rule gives $1\cdot x+x\cdot1=2x$, correct. Geometrically, if a rectangle has sides $u$ and $v$ that both grow, the area grows by a strip along each side: $u'v+uv'$.
@p **A safe layout.** Write four things in a small table before assembling: $u=\ \ $, $v=\ \ $, $u'=\ \ $, $v'=\ \ $. Each of $u'$ and $v'$ may need its own chain rule. Then assemble $u'v+uv'$ and **factorise** the common factors. Factorising is not optional: to find stationary points you need a product of factors set equal to zero.
@p **Factorising tips.** With exponentials, factor out the exponential with the smaller power: $2xe^{3x}+3x^2e^{3x}=xe^{3x}(2+3x)$. With logs and powers of $x$: $3x^2\ln x+x^2=x^2(3\ln x+1)$. Remember $e^{kx}>0$ always, so that factor never gives a stationary point.
@ex Product of a power and an exponential (full working)
Q: Differentiate $y=x^2e^{3x}$ and fully factorise.
S: **Set up:** $u=x^2$, $v=e^{3x}$.
S: **Derivatives:** $u'=2x$; $v'=3e^{3x}$ (chain rule).
S: **Assemble $u'v+uv'$:** $2x\cdot e^{3x}+x^2\cdot3e^{3x}$.
S: **Identify common factors:** both terms contain $x$ and $e^{3x}$.
S: **Factorise:** $xe^{3x}(2+3x)$.
A: $\dfrac{dy}{dx}=xe^{3x}(3x+2)$
@ex Stationary points with the product rule (full working)
Q: Find the stationary points of $y=x^2e^{-x}$.
S: **Set up:** $u=x^2$, $u'=2x$; $v=e^{-x}$, $v'=-e^{-x}$.
S: **Assemble:** $y'=2xe^{-x}+x^2(-e^{-x})=2xe^{-x}-x^2e^{-x}$.
S: **Factorise:** $y'=xe^{-x}(2-x)$.
S: **Set to zero:** a product is zero when a factor is zero. $x=0$, or $2-x=0\Rightarrow x=2$. $e^{-x}$ is never zero.
S: **Find $y$-values:** $y(0)=0$; $y(2)=4e^{-2}\approx0.541$.
S: **Nature by sign test:** $y'$ is negative for $x<0$, positive for $0<x<2$, negative for $x>2$. So $(0,0)$ is a minimum and $(2,4e^{-2})$ is a maximum.
A: Minimum $(0,0)$; maximum $\left(2,\ 4e^{-2}\right)$
@ex Product with a log (full working)
Q: Find the stationary point of $y=x\ln x$ ($x>0$).
S: **Set up:** $u=x$, $u'=1$; $v=\ln x$, $v'=\frac1x$.
S: **Assemble:** $y'=1\cdot\ln x+x\cdot\frac1x=\ln x+1$.
S: **Set to zero:** $\ln x=-1\Rightarrow x=e^{-1}$.
S: **$y$-value:** $y=e^{-1}\ln e^{-1}=e^{-1}\times(-1)=-e^{-1}$.
S: **Nature:** $y''=\frac1x>0$, so it is a minimum.
A: Minimum at $\left(e^{-1},\,-e^{-1}\right)$
@ex Product of three factors (full working)
Q: Differentiate $y=x^2e^x\sin x$.
S: **Extend the product rule:** for $uvw$, $(uvw)'=u'vw+uv'w+uvw'$ (differentiate one factor at a time, keeping the others).
S: **Derivatives:** $u=x^2$, $u'=2x$; $v=e^x$, $v'=e^x$; $w=\sin x$, $w'=\cos x$.
S: **Assemble:** $2x\,e^x\sin x+x^2e^x\sin x+x^2e^x\cos x$.
S: **Factorise $xe^x$:** $xe^x\big(2\sin x+x\sin x+x\cos x\big)$.
A: $xe^x\left(2\sin x+x\sin x+x\cos x\right)$
@warn $(uv)'\neq u'v'$. Also take care with signs inside $v'$: $\frac{d}{dx}e^{-x}=-e^{-x}$ and $\frac{d}{dx}\cos x=-\sin x$. Write $u'$ and $v'$ separately first.
@p **Exam tip.** In technology-free papers you must show the product-rule line before simplifying. A bare final answer with an error gets no method mark.
@check Product rule | Differentiate $y=x^3e^{2x}$.
A: x^2e^(2x)(2x+3)
ALT: e^(2x)x^2(2x+3) || 3x^2e^(2x)+2x^3e^(2x) || x^2e^(2x)(3+2x)
AD: $x^2e^{2x}(2x+3)$
SOL: $u=x^3$, $u'=3x^2$; $v=e^{2x}$, $v'=2e^{2x}$. || $u'v+uv'=3x^2e^{2x}+2x^3e^{2x}$. || Both terms share $x^2e^{2x}$. || Factorise: $x^2e^{2x}(3+2x)$.
H: Write $u$, $v$, $u'$, $v'$. || $v'$ needs the chain rule. || Factor out $x^2e^{2x}$.
@check Product with trig | Differentiate $y=x\sin x$.
A: sinx+xcosx
ALT: sin(x)+xcos(x) || xcosx+sinx
AD: $\sin x+x\cos x$
SOL: $u=x$, $u'=1$; $v=\sin x$, $v'=\cos x$. || Product rule: $u'v+uv'=1\cdot\sin x+x\cos x$. || There is no common factor to take out. || So $\frac{dy}{dx}=\sin x+x\cos x$.
H: Identify $u$ and $v$. || $u'=1$. || Assemble $u'v+uv'$.
@check Gradient | Find the gradient of $y=xe^{x}$ at $x=1$. Give an exact answer.
A: 2e
AD: $2e$
SOL: $y'=1\cdot e^x+x\cdot e^x=e^x(1+x)$. || Substitute $x=1$: $e^1(1+1)$. || $=2e$. || The gradient is $2e\approx5.44$.
H: Product rule with $u=x$, $v=e^x$. || Factorise $e^x$. || Substitute $x=1$.
@check Stationary point | Find the $x$-coordinate of the stationary point of $y=xe^{-2x}$.
A: 1/2
ALT: 0.5 || x=1/2
AD: $x=\tfrac12$
SOL: $u=x$, $u'=1$; $v=e^{-2x}$, $v'=-2e^{-2x}$. || $y'=e^{-2x}-2xe^{-2x}=e^{-2x}(1-2x)$. || $e^{-2x}\neq0$, so $1-2x=0$. || $x=\frac12$.
H: Product rule. || Factor out $e^{-2x}$. || Set the other factor to zero.
@check Product with log | Differentiate $y=x^2\ln x$.
A: x(2lnx+1)
ALT: 2xlnx+x || x(2ln(x)+1) || 2xln(x)+x
AD: $x(2\ln x+1)$
SOL: $u=x^2$, $u'=2x$; $v=\ln x$, $v'=\frac1x$. || $u'v+uv'=2x\ln x+x^2\cdot\frac1x$. || Simplify: $2x\ln x+x$. || Factorise: $x(2\ln x+1)$.
H: $v'=\frac1x$. || $x^2\cdot\frac1x=x$. || Factor out $x$.
