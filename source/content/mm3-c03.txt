## 3.2 | The chain rule
@p A **composite function** is a function inside a function, $y=f(g(x))$. Let $u=g(x)$. Then $y=f(u)$ and
@key $$\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{dy}{du}\cdot\frac{du}{dx}\qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad \big[f(g(x))\big]'=f'(g(x))\,g'(x)$$
@p In words: differentiate the outside (leaving the inside alone), then multiply by the derivative of the inside. Special case: $\frac{d}{dx}(ax+b)^n=na(ax+b)^{n-1}$.
@ex Chain rule
Q: Differentiate $y=(3x^2+1)^5$.
S: Let $u=3x^2+1$, so $y=u^5$. $\frac{dy}{du}=5u^4$, $\frac{du}{dx}=6x$.
S: $\frac{dy}{dx}=5(3x^2+1)^4\cdot6x=30x(3x^2+1)^4$.
A: $30x(3x^2+1)^4$
@ex Chain rule with a root
Q: Differentiate $y=\sqrt{2x-1}$.
S: Write $y=(2x-1)^{1/2}$. Then $y'=\frac12(2x-1)^{-1/2}\cdot2$.
S: $y'=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2x-1}}$.
A: $\dfrac1{\sqrt{2x-1}}$
@warn Forgetting to multiply by the inside derivative is the number one chain rule error. After you differentiate, ask: "did I multiply by $u'$?"

## 3.3 | The product rule
@key $$\frac{d}{dx}(uv)=u'v+uv'$$
@p Use it when two functions containing $x$ are **multiplied**. Choose $u$ and $v$, find $u'$ and $v'$ separately, assemble, then **factorise** the answer.
@ex Product rule
Q: Differentiate $y=x^2e^{3x}$.
S: $u=x^2$, $u'=2x$; $v=e^{3x}$, $v'=3e^{3x}$.
S: $y'=2xe^{3x}+x^2\cdot3e^{3x}=xe^{3x}(2+3x)$.
A: $y'=xe^{3x}(3x+2)$
@ex Stationary point using the product rule
Q: Find the stationary point of $y=x\ln x$ for $x>0$.
S: $y'=1\cdot\ln x+x\cdot\frac1x=\ln x+1$.
S: $\ln x+1=0\Rightarrow x=e^{-1}$, and $y=e^{-1}\ln e^{-1}=-e^{-1}$.
A: $\left(e^{-1},-e^{-1}\right)$
@warn $(uv)'$ is **not** $u'v'$. Check with $x\cdot x$: the derivative of $x^2$ is $2x$, but $u'v'$ would give $1$.

## 3.4 | The quotient rule
@key $$\frac{d}{dx}\left(\frac uv\right)=\frac{u'v-uv'}{v^2}$$
@p The numerator order matters: it is "bottom times derivative of top **minus** top times derivative of bottom", all over bottom squared. If the denominator is a single power of $x$, rewrite as a product with a negative index instead.
@ex Quotient rule
Q: Differentiate $y=\dfrac{x}{x^2+1}$.
S: $u=x$, $u'=1$; $v=x^2+1$, $v'=2x$.
S: $y'=\frac{1\cdot(x^2+1)-x\cdot2x}{(x^2+1)^2}=\frac{1-x^2}{(x^2+1)^2}$.
A: $\dfrac{1-x^2}{(x^2+1)^2}$
@ex Quotient rule with e
Q: Differentiate $y=\dfrac{e^{2x}}{x}$.
S: $u=e^{2x}$, $u'=2e^{2x}$; $v=x$, $v'=1$.
S: $y'=\frac{2xe^{2x}-e^{2x}}{x^2}=\frac{e^{2x}(2x-1)}{x^2}$.
A: $\dfrac{e^{2x}(2x-1)}{x^2}$
@warn Swapping the numerator terms flips the sign of your answer. Memorise the order, then check it with a simple case like $\frac{x}{1}$.

## 3.5 | Applications of differentiation
@p **Tangent:** at $x=a$ the gradient is $f'(a)$, so $y-f(a)=f'(a)(x-a)$. **Normal:** perpendicular to the tangent, gradient $-\frac1{f'(a)}$.
@p **Kinematics:** if $x(t)$ is position, velocity is $v=\frac{dx}{dt}$ and acceleration is $a=\frac{dv}{dt}$. The particle is at rest when $v=0$.
@p **Optimisation (preview):** to maximise or minimise a quantity, write it as a function of one variable, set the derivative to zero, then confirm it is a max or min.
@ex Normal to a curve
Q: Find the equation of the normal to $y=\ln x$ at $x=1$.
S: $y'=\frac1x$, so at $x=1$ the tangent gradient is $1$. The normal gradient is $-1$.
S: Point $(1,0)$. Normal: $y-0=-1(x-1)$.
A: $y=-x+1$
@ex Kinematics
Q: A particle has $x=t^2e^{-t}$ for $t\ge0$. When is it at rest?
S: $v=2te^{-t}-t^2e^{-t}=te^{-t}(2-t)$.
S: $v=0\Rightarrow t=0$ or $t=2$.
A: $t=0$ and $t=2$
