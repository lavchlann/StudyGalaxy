## 1.2 | Review of complex numbers
@p The imaginary unit satisfies $i^2=-1$. A complex number is $z=a+bi$ with real part $\operatorname{Re}(z)=a$ and imaginary part $\operatorname{Im}(z)=b$. Plot it on the **Argand plane** at $(a,b)$.
@p The **conjugate** is $\bar z=a-bi$. Then $z\bar z=a^2+b^2$ is real and positive, and this is how you divide: multiply top and bottom by the conjugate of the denominator.
@key $|z|=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}$ (modulus); $\bar{z_1z_2}=\bar z_1\bar z_2$; $|z_1z_2|=|z_1||z_2|$; the roots of a real polynomial come in **conjugate pairs**.
@ex Dividing
Q: Write $\dfrac{3+i}{1-2i}$ in the form $a+bi$.
S: Multiply by $\frac{1+2i}{1+2i}$: numerator $(3+i)(1+2i)=3+6i+i+2i^2=1+7i$.
S: Denominator: $(1-2i)(1+2i)=1+4=5$.
A: $\frac15+\frac75i$
@ex Solving a quadratic
Q: Solve $z^2-4z+13=0$.
S: $z=\frac{4\pm\sqrt{16-52}}2=\frac{4\pm6i}{2}$.
A: $z=2\pm3i$
@warn $\sqrt{-36}=6i$, not $\pm6i$. The $\pm$ comes from the quadratic formula, not from the root.

## 1.3 | Polar form and arithmetic
@p Write $z=r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)=r\operatorname{cis}\theta$, where $r=|z|$ and $\theta=\operatorname{Arg}z\in(-\pi,\pi]$ is the **principal argument**. Find $\theta$ from the quadrant, using $\tan^{-1}\left|\frac ba\right|$ as the reference angle.
@key **Multiplication:** $r_1\operatorname{cis}\theta_1\cdot r_2\operatorname{cis}\theta_2=r_1r_2\operatorname{cis}(\theta_1+\theta_2)$. **Division:** $\dfrac{r_1}{r_2}\operatorname{cis}(\theta_1-\theta_2)$. Multiplying by $\operatorname{cis}\phi$ **rotates** by $\phi$.
@ex To polar form
Q: Write $z=-1+i$ in polar form.
S: $r=\sqrt2$. In quadrant 2, reference angle $\frac\pi4$, so $\theta=\pi-\frac\pi4=\frac{3\pi}4$.
A: $\sqrt2\operatorname{cis}\frac{3\pi}4$
@ex Multiplying in polar form
Q: $z_1=2\operatorname{cis}\frac\pi3$, $z_2=3\operatorname{cis}\frac\pi2$. Find $z_1z_2$ in Cartesian form.
S: $6\operatorname{cis}\frac{5\pi}6=6\left(-\frac{\sqrt3}2+\frac12i\right)$.
A: $-3\sqrt3+3i$
@warn Check the quadrant. Using $\tan^{-1}\frac ba$ blindly gives the wrong angle for $-1+i$ (it returns $-\frac\pi4$).

## 1.4 | De Moivre's theorem and roots
@key $$\big[r\operatorname{cis}\theta\big]^n=r^n\operatorname{cis}(n\theta)$$
@p To find the **$n$th roots** of $w=R\operatorname{cis}\alpha$: write $w=R\operatorname{cis}(\alpha+2k\pi)$, take $z=R^{1/n}\operatorname{cis}\frac{\alpha+2k\pi}n$ for $k=0,1,\dots,n-1$. The roots lie on a circle of radius $R^{1/n}$, evenly spaced by $\frac{2\pi}n$, forming a regular $n$-gon. For $z^n=1$ these are the **roots of unity**.
@ex Power
Q: Evaluate $(1+i)^8$.
S: $1+i=\sqrt2\operatorname{cis}\frac\pi4$. So $(1+i)^8=(\sqrt2)^8\operatorname{cis}2\pi=16$.
A: $16$
@ex Roots
Q: Solve $z^3=8$.
S: $8=8\operatorname{cis}(2k\pi)$, so $z=2\operatorname{cis}\frac{2k\pi}3$ for $k=0,1,2$.
S: $z=2,\ 2\operatorname{cis}\frac{2\pi}3=-1+\sqrt3i,\ 2\operatorname{cis}\frac{4\pi}3=-1-\sqrt3i$.
A: $z=2,\ -1\pm\sqrt3i$
@warn Give exactly $n$ roots. Dropping $2k\pi$ gives only one of them.

## 1.5 | Factorising polynomials
@p **Factor theorem:** $z=a$ is a root of $P(z)$ if and only if $(z-a)$ is a factor. **Conjugate root theorem:** if $P$ has real coefficients and $a+bi$ is a root, so is $a-bi$, giving the real quadratic factor $z^2-2az+(a^2+b^2)$. A degree-$n$ polynomial has exactly $n$ complex roots (counted with multiplicity).
@ex Using the conjugate pair
Q: $P(z)=z^3-5z^2+17z-13$ has root $z=2+3i$. Factorise fully.
S: $2-3i$ is also a root. The quadratic factor is $z^2-4z+13$.
S: Divide: $P(z)=(z^2-4z+13)(z-1)$.
A: $(z-1)(z-2-3i)(z-2+3i)$
