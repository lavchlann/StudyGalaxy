## 1.2 | Limits
@p A **limit** describes the value a function approaches as the input gets close to a point, even if the function never actually reaches it. We write $\lim_{x\to a} f(x)=L$. For most nice functions the limit is just the value, so $\lim_{x\to 2}(x^2+1)=5$. Limits become interesting when direct substitution gives $\tfrac{0}{0}$, because then you must simplify first.
@p Because $x$ can approach $a$ from the left ($x\to a^-$) or the right ($x\to a^+$), a limit only exists when both sides agree. Limits obey the usual laws: the limit of a sum, product or quotient is the sum, product or quotient of the limits (as long as you do not divide by a limit of zero).
@ex Limit that needs factorising
Q: Evaluate $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 3}\frac{x^2-9}{x-3}$.
S: Substituting $x=3$ gives $\tfrac{0}{0}$, so factorise the numerator: $\dfrac{(x-3)(x+3)}{x-3}$.
S: For $x\neq 3$ the factor $x-3$ cancels, leaving $x+3$.
S: Now substitute: $3+3=6$.
A: $6$
@p The idea behind all of calculus is the **derivative as a limit**. The gradient of the tangent at $x$ is the limit of the gradient of a chord as the chord shrinks to a point:
@key $$f'(x)=\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}$$
@ex Derivative from first principles
Q: Use first principles to find $f'(x)$ for $f(x)=x^2$.
S: $\dfrac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}=\dfrac{(x+h)^2-x^2}{h}=\dfrac{2xh+h^2}{h}$.
S: Divide by $h$: $2x+h$.
S: Let $h\to 0$: $f'(x)=2x$.
A: $f'(x)=2x$
@p You can also **estimate** a limit numerically. Evaluate the function at values closer and closer to the point on both sides and watch where the outputs head. This is how the number $e$ is first met: the values of $\left(1+\tfrac1n\right)^n$ climb towards $2.71828\ldots$ as $n$ grows, and we define that limit to be $e$.
@warn A limit of $\tfrac00$ does not mean the limit does not exist, and it does not mean the answer is $0$. It means you have not simplified enough yet.

## 1.3 | The exponential function
@p An exponential function has the variable in the **index**: $y=a^x$ with $a>0$, $a\neq 1$. Every such graph passes through $(0,1)$, has the horizontal asymptote $y=0$, and is always positive. If $a>1$ it grows (the graph rises to the right). If $0<a<1$ it decays (the graph falls to the right).
@p The special base is **Euler's number** $e\approx 2.71828$. It is the unique base for which the gradient of the curve at every point equals the height of the curve. That is why $y=e^x$ is the star of calculus.
@key For $y=ae^{b(x-h)}+k$: the **horizontal asymptote** is $y=k$; $a$ stretches vertically (negative $a$ reflects in the $x$-axis); $b$ stretches horizontally (negative $b$ reflects in the $y$-axis); $h$ shifts right and $k$ shifts up.
@ex Sketching a transformed exponential
Q: Sketch $y=2e^{-x}+1$. State the asymptote, the $y$-intercept and the range.
S: The base graph $y=e^x$ is reflected in the $y$-axis (because of $-x$), stretched by 2 vertically, then shifted up by 1.
S: Horizontal asymptote: $y=1$.
S: $y$-intercept: $x=0$ gives $y=2e^0+1=3$, so the point is $(0,3)$.
S: The graph is always above the asymptote, so the range is $y>1$. There is no $x$-intercept.
A: Asymptote $y=1$, $y$-intercept $(0,3)$, range $(1,\infty)$.
@p To find an **$x$-intercept**, set $y=0$ and solve. Since $e^{\text{anything}}>0$, you must be able to isolate $e^{\ldots}$ equal to a positive number or the graph has no $x$-intercept.
@warn $e^{x+2}$ is not $e^x+2$. A shift inside the index moves the graph left or right. A number added outside moves it up or down, and only the second one moves the asymptote.
@int expfun | Slide $a$ and watch the gradient of $y=a^x$ at $x=0$. When $a=e$ the gradient equals the height.

## 1.4 | Differentiation of exponential functions
@key $$\frac{d}{dx}\left(e^x\right)=e^x\qquad\text{and}\qquad \frac{d}{dx}\left(e^{f(x)}\right)=f'(x)\,e^{f(x)}$$
@p The second rule is the chain rule in action: differentiate the **inside** and multiply by the original exponential. So $\frac{d}{dx}e^{3x}=3e^{3x}$, $\frac{d}{dx}e^{x^2}=2xe^{x^2}$ and $\frac{d}{dx}e^{-2x+1}=-2e^{-2x+1}$.
@ex Differentiating exponentials
Q: Differentiate $y=5e^{4x}-e^{-x^2}$.
S: First term: $5\times 4e^{4x}=20e^{4x}$.
S: Second term: the inside is $-x^2$ with derivative $-2x$, so $\frac{d}{dx}e^{-x^2}=-2xe^{-x^2}$. Because of the minus sign in front, this term contributes $+2xe^{-x^2}$.
S: $y'=20e^{4x}+2xe^{-x^2}$.
A: $y'=20e^{4x}+2xe^{-x^2}$
@ex Equation of a tangent
Q: Find the equation of the tangent to $y=e^{2x}$ at $x=0$.
S: $y'=2e^{2x}$, so at $x=0$ the gradient is $2$.
S: The point is $(0,e^0)=(0,1)$.
S: Tangent: $y-1=2(x-0)$, so $y=2x+1$.
A: $y=2x+1$
@warn Do not write $\frac{d}{dx}e^{3x}=3xe^{3x-1}$. The power rule does not apply to $e^x$, because $x$ is in the index, not the base.

## 1.5 | The logarithmic function
@p A **logarithm** answers the question "what power?". $\log_a(x)=y$ means $a^y=x$. The **natural logarithm** is the log to base $e$: $\ln x=\log_e x$. It is the inverse of $e^x$, so $\ln(e^x)=x$ and $e^{\ln x}=x$.
@key **Log laws** (any base): $\log(xy)=\log x+\log y$; $\log\!\left(\tfrac xy\right)=\log x-\log y$; $\log(x^n)=n\log x$; $\log_a 1=0$; $\log_a a=1$.
@p Because $\ln x$ is the inverse of $e^x$, its graph is the reflection of $y=e^x$ in the line $y=x$. So $y=\ln x$ has domain $x>0$, range all reals, a **vertical asymptote** at $x=0$, and passes through $(1,0)$.
@ex Simplifying with log laws
Q: Write $2\ln 3+\ln 5-\ln 15$ as a single logarithm and simplify.
S: $2\ln 3=\ln 9$, so the expression is $\ln 9+\ln 5-\ln 15$.
S: $\ln 9+\ln 5=\ln 45$, and $\ln 45-\ln 15=\ln\frac{45}{15}=\ln 3$.
A: $\ln 3$
@ex Sketching a transformed log graph
Q: Describe $y=\ln(x-2)+1$.
S: Shift $y=\ln x$ right by 2 and up by 1.
S: Vertical asymptote $x=2$, domain $x>2$.
S: $x$-intercept: $\ln(x-2)=-1\Rightarrow x-2=e^{-1}\Rightarrow x=2+e^{-1}\approx 2.37$.
A: Asymptote $x=2$, domain $x>2$, $x$-intercept $2+e^{-1}$.
@warn $\ln(x+y)$ is not $\ln x+\ln y$. The log of a sum has no simple rule, and $\ln(x)\ln(y)$ is not $\ln(xy)$.
@int logfun | Move the point on $y=e^x$ and watch its mirror image on $y=\ln x$ across the line $y=x$.

## 1.6 | Solving equations
@p **Equations with $e$.** Isolate the exponential, then take $\ln$ of both sides. For $3e^{2x}=18$: divide to get $e^{2x}=6$, take $\ln$: $2x=\ln 6$, so $x=\tfrac12\ln 6$.
@p **Quadratics in $e^x$.** If you see $e^{2x}$ and $e^x$ together, substitute $u=e^x$ and solve the quadratic. Remember $u=e^x$ must be positive, so reject any $u\le 0$.
@ex A quadratic in $e^x$
Q: Solve $e^{2x}-5e^x+6=0$.
S: Let $u=e^x$, so $u^2-5u+6=0$, giving $(u-2)(u-3)=0$.
S: $e^x=2\Rightarrow x=\ln 2$ and $e^x=3\Rightarrow x=\ln 3$.
A: $x=\ln 2$ or $x=\ln 3$
@p **Equations with $\ln$.** Combine logs into one, rewrite in index form, then solve. Always **check the domain**: any solution that makes a log argument zero or negative is rejected.
@ex A log equation with a domain check
Q: Solve $\ln(x)+\ln(x-2)=\ln 3$.
S: Combine: $\ln\big(x(x-2)\big)=\ln 3$, so $x^2-2x=3$.
S: $x^2-2x-3=0\Rightarrow (x-3)(x+1)=0$.
S: $x=-1$ makes $\ln x$ undefined, so reject it.
A: $x=3$
@warn Rejecting solutions is part of the answer. In an exam, write "reject, since $\ln$ is undefined for negative arguments" and you keep the mark.

## 1.7 | Differentiation of logarithmic functions
@key $$\frac{d}{dx}\big(\ln x\big)=\frac1x\quad(x>0)\qquad\text{and}\qquad \frac{d}{dx}\big(\ln f(x)\big)=\frac{f'(x)}{f(x)}$$
@p The second rule says: derivative of the inside **over** the inside. So $\frac{d}{dx}\ln(3x+1)=\frac{3}{3x+1}$ and $\frac{d}{dx}\ln(x^2+4)=\frac{2x}{x^2+4}$.
@p A useful shortcut is to simplify with log laws **before** differentiating. $\ln(x^5)=5\ln x$, so its derivative is $\tfrac5x$, much quicker than using the chain rule.
@ex Differentiating logs
Q: Differentiate $y=\ln\!\big((2x+1)^3\big)$ in two ways.
S: Chain rule: $\frac{f'}{f}=\frac{3(2x+1)^2\cdot 2}{(2x+1)^3}=\frac{6}{2x+1}$.
S: Log law first: $y=3\ln(2x+1)$, so $y'=3\cdot\frac{2}{2x+1}=\frac{6}{2x+1}$.
A: $\dfrac{6}{2x+1}$
@warn $\frac{d}{dx}\ln(3x)=\frac1x$, not $\frac{1}{3x}$. The inside derivative is $3$, so the answer is $\frac{3}{3x}=\frac1x$.
@tb rec=1,4,6,10,11 ext=14,16,17,19 | Exercise 1.7 practises $\ln f(x)$ derivatives, tangents to log curves, and using log laws first.

## 1.8 | Modelling and solving with exponential and logarithmic functions
@p **Growth and decay.** A quantity that changes at a rate proportional to its size follows $N=N_0e^{kt}$. If $k>0$ it grows; if $k<0$ it decays. $N_0$ is the starting amount (at $t=0$). The rate of change is $\frac{dN}{dt}=kN$.
@p **Half-life.** The time for a decaying quantity to halve. Solve $\tfrac12N_0=N_0e^{kt}$ to get $t=\dfrac{\ln 2}{-k}$.
@p **Decay to a limit.** Many real models approach a constant, such as cooling to room temperature: $T=T_\infty+Ae^{kt}$ with $k<0$. The horizontal asymptote $T=T_\infty$ is the long-term value.
@ex Exponential decay
Q: A drug amount is $A=80e^{-0.2t}$ mg after $t$ hours. Find the amount after 3 hours and how fast it is changing then.
S: $A(3)=80e^{-0.6}\approx 43.9$ mg.
S: $\frac{dA}{dt}=-0.2A$, so at $t=3$ the rate is $-0.2\times 43.9\approx -8.78$ mg/h.
A: About $43.9$ mg, decreasing at about $8.78$ mg per hour.
@ex Time to reach a value
Q: When does $A=80e^{-0.2t}$ fall to $10$ mg?
S: $10=80e^{-0.2t}\Rightarrow e^{-0.2t}=\tfrac18$.
S: $-0.2t=\ln\tfrac18=-\ln 8\Rightarrow t=5\ln 8\approx 10.4$ hours.
A: $t=5\ln 8\approx 10.4$ hours
@warn Always state units and interpret the sign. A negative rate of change means the quantity is decreasing, not that the answer is "wrong".
