## 4.1 | Newton's law of gravitation
@p Any two masses attract each other with equal and opposite forces along the line joining their centres. The force depends on the product of the masses and falls with the **square of the distance** between centres. $G=6.67\times10^{-11}\ \text{N m}^2\text{kg}^{-2}$.
@key $$F=\frac{Gm_1m_2}{r^2}$$
@ex Two cars
Q: Two $1.0\times10^3\ \text{kg}$ cars are $2.0\ \text{m}$ apart. Find the gravitational force between them.
S: $F=\frac{6.67\times10^{-11}(1000)(1000)}{2.0^2}=1.67\times10^{-5}\ \text{N}$.
A: $1.67\times10^{-5}\ \text{N}$, far too small to notice.
@warn $r$ is the distance between **centres of mass**, not surfaces. For an object at height $h$ above a planet of radius $R$, use $r=R+h$.

## 4.2 | Gravitational field strength
@p The **gravitational field strength** is the force per unit mass: $g=\dfrac{F}{m}=\dfrac{GM}{r^2}$ (in $\text{N kg}^{-1}$, the same as $\text{m s}^{-2}$). It points toward the centre of the mass, and **doubling the distance divides it by 4**. Near Earth's surface $g\approx9.8\ \text{N kg}^{-1}$, which can be confirmed from $G$, $M_E=5.97\times10^{24}\ \text{kg}$ and $R_E=6.37\times10^6\ \text{m}$.
@ex Field strength
Q: Find $g$ at the surface of Mars ($M=6.4\times10^{23}\ \text{kg}$, $R=3.4\times10^6\ \text{m}$).
S: $g=\frac{GM}{R^2}=\frac{6.67\times10^{-11}(6.4\times10^{23})}{(3.4\times10^6)^2}=3.69\ \text{N kg}^{-1}$.
A: $3.69\ \text{N kg}^{-1}$
@ex At altitude
Q: Find $g$ at $400\ \text{km}$ above Earth's surface.
S: $r=6.37\times10^6+4.0\times10^5=6.77\times10^6\ \text{m}$.
S: $g=\frac{6.67\times10^{-11}(5.97\times10^{24})}{(6.77\times10^6)^2}=8.69\ \text{N kg}^{-1}$.
A: $8.69\ \text{N kg}^{-1}$, only slightly less than at the surface.

## 4.3 | Orbits and Kepler's laws
@p For a satellite in a circular orbit, **gravity is the centripetal force**: $\dfrac{GMm}{r^2}=\dfrac{mv^2}{r}$ so $v=\sqrt{\dfrac{GM}{r}}$ (independent of the satellite's mass). With $v=\frac{2\pi r}{T}$ this gives **Kepler's third law**: $\dfrac{T^2}{r^3}=\dfrac{4\pi^2}{GM}$, a constant for all orbits around the same central mass.
@key $$v=\sqrt{\frac{GM}{r}},\qquad \frac{r^3}{T^2}=\frac{GM}{4\pi^2}$$
@ex Space station
Q: Find the orbital speed and period of a satellite $400\ \text{km}$ above Earth.
S: $r=6.77\times10^6$; $v=\sqrt{\frac{GM}{r}}=\sqrt{\frac{3.98\times10^{14}}{6.77\times10^6}}=7.67\times10^3\ \text{m s}^{-1}$.
S: $T=\frac{2\pi r}{v}=5.55\times10^3\ \text{s}=92.4\ \text{min}$.
A: $7.67\ \text{km s}^{-1}$ and $92\ \text{min}$.
@p **Kepler's laws.** (1) Orbits are ellipses with the central body at one focus. (2) A line from the central body sweeps equal areas in equal times, so orbiting bodies move fastest when closest. (3) $T^2\propto r^3$. Higher orbits are **slower** and take **longer**.

## 4.4 | Geostationary satellites and weightlessness
@p A **geostationary** satellite has a period of 24 hours, orbits above the equator in the same direction as Earth's rotation, and so stays above one point. Its radius is found from $r^3=\frac{GMT^2}{4\pi^2}\approx4.22\times10^7\ \text{m}$ from Earth's centre, about $3.59\times10^7\ \text{m}$ above the surface.
@p **Apparent weightlessness.** An astronaut in orbit is in **free fall** around Earth. Gravity still acts (about $90\%$ of surface value at ISS altitude), but there is no contact force from the floor, so no sensation of weight.
@warn Orbiting astronauts do not float because gravity is absent. They float because they and the spacecraft fall together.

@p **Constants and rounding.** Use $G=6.67\times10^{-11}$, $M_E=5.97\times10^{24}\ \text{kg}$, $R_E=6.37\times10^6\ \text{m}$. Keep full precision until the end. Convert km to m before substituting.
@p **Proportional reasoning.** Many questions are asked as ratios. If $r$ doubles, $F$ and $g$ become $\frac14$, $v$ becomes $\frac1{\sqrt2}$ and $T$ becomes $2\sqrt2$ times larger. Writing the proportion with symbols is quicker and safer than recalculating.
