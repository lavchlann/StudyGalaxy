## 4.4
@p **What this topic is about.** Using everything about derivatives to draw an accurate sketch of a function, with all key features labelled. Sketching shows that you understand how a function behaves, and graph questions often ask you to label exact coordinates and justify their nature.
@key **Sketching checklist:** (1) **Domain** and any restrictions. (2) **Intercepts:** $y$-intercept at $x=0$; $x$-intercepts from $y=0$. (3) **Stationary points** from $f'(x)=0$, with their nature. (4) **Points of inflection** from $f''(x)=0$ with a sign change. (5) **Asymptotes** and **end behaviour** (what happens as $x\to\pm\infty$). (6) Join smoothly, respecting the concavity, and **label** every feature with exact coordinates.
@p **End behaviour.** For a polynomial, the highest-power term decides: $-x^3$ goes to $-\infty$ as $x\to\infty$ and to $+\infty$ as $x\to-\infty$. For $xe^{-x}$, the exponential wins as $x\to\infty$, so $y\to0$ (the $x$-axis is an asymptote). For $\ln x$, there is a vertical asymptote at $x=0$.
@p **Touching versus crossing.** A repeated factor $(x-a)^2$ means the graph touches the $x$-axis at $a$ (a turning point there); a single factor means it crosses; a cubed factor $(x-a)^3$ means a stationary point of inflection on the axis.
@p **Labelling in an exam.** Write coordinates next to each feature, e.g. "max $(1,4)$", "PoI $(0,2)$". Draw asymptotes dashed with their equations. Make the curve pass exactly through labelled points with the correct shape (no extra wiggles).
@ex Full sketch of a cubic (full working)
Q: Find all key features of $y=-x^3+3x+2$ needed for a sketch.
S: **$y$-intercept:** $x=0\Rightarrow y=2$, so $(0,2)$.
S: **$x$-intercepts:** test $x=-1$: $1-3+2=0$ ✓, so $(x+1)$ is a factor. Dividing gives $-x^3+3x+2=-(x+1)^2(x-2)$. So $x=-1$ (repeated, the graph touches) and $x=2$ (crosses).
S: **Stationary points:** $y'=-3x^2+3=0\Rightarrow x=\pm1$. $y(-1)=1-3+2=0$; $y(1)=-1+3+2=4$.
S: **Nature:** $y''=-6x$. At $x=-1$, $y''=6>0$: minimum $(-1,0)$. At $x=1$, $y''=-6<0$: maximum $(1,4)$.
S: **Inflection:** $y''=0$ at $x=0$ with a sign change, so $(0,2)$ is a point of inflection.
S: **End behaviour:** the leading term $-x^3$ means $y\to-\infty$ as $x\to\infty$ and $y\to\infty$ as $x\to-\infty$.
A: Min $(-1,0)$ touching the axis, max $(1,4)$, inflection $(0,2)$, crosses at $(2,0)$; falls to the right
@ex Sketch of an exponential product (full working)
Q: Find the key features of $f(x)=xe^{-x}$ for $x\ge0$.
S: **Intercept:** $f(0)=0$; and $f(x)=0$ only when $x=0$ (since $e^{-x}>0$). So the graph starts at the origin.
S: **Stationary point:** $f'(x)=e^{-x}(1-x)=0\Rightarrow x=1$, $f(1)=e^{-1}\approx0.368$.
S: **Nature:** $f''(x)=e^{-x}(x-2)$; $f''(1)=-e^{-1}<0$: local maximum $\left(1,e^{-1}\right)$.
S: **Inflection:** $f''=0$ at $x=2$ with a sign change: $\left(2,2e^{-2}\right)\approx(2,0.271)$.
S: **End behaviour:** as $x\to\infty$, $xe^{-x}\to0$ (the exponential decays faster than $x$ grows), so $y=0$ is a horizontal asymptote.
A: Starts at $(0,0)$, max $(1,e^{-1})$, inflection $(2,2e^{-2})$, approaches $y=0$
@ex Sketch involving a log (full working)
Q: Find the key features of $y=x-2\ln x$.
S: **Domain:** $\ln x$ requires $x>0$.
S: **Vertical asymptote:** as $x\to0^+$, $-2\ln x\to+\infty$, so $y\to+\infty$: the $y$-axis is an asymptote.
S: **Stationary point:** $y'=1-\frac2x=0\Rightarrow x=2$, $y=2-2\ln2\approx0.614$.
S: **Nature:** $y''=\frac{2}{x^2}>0$ for all $x>0$, so a minimum, and the graph is concave up everywhere (no inflection).
S: **$x$-intercepts:** the minimum value $0.614$ is positive, so the graph never reaches the $x$-axis.
S: **End behaviour:** as $x\to\infty$, $x$ grows faster than $2\ln x$, so $y\to\infty$.
A: Domain $x>0$, asymptote $x=0$, minimum $(2,\,2-2\ln2)$, concave up, no $x$-intercepts
@warn Do not draw a turning point where there is only a point of inflection, and do not let a curve cross its asymptote unless your working shows it does. Always label exact coordinates, not just approximate ones.
@p **Exam tip.** Use your working as a table of features before drawing. A neat sketch that matches a correct table usually earns full marks even if the drawing is not perfectly to scale.

## 4.5
@p **What this topic is about.** Optimisation means finding the greatest or least value of a quantity: maximum area, minimum cost, shortest time, maximum profit. These questions are worded, multi-step and appear in nearly every external exam. The calculus is usually easy; the setup is where marks are won or lost.
@key **Optimisation method:** (1) Draw a diagram and **define variables** with units. (2) Write the quantity to optimise using two variables. (3) Use the **constraint** (fixed perimeter, volume, cost) to rewrite it in **one** variable. (4) State the **domain** from the context. (5) Differentiate and solve $=0$. (6) **Justify** max or min (second derivative or sign test) and check the **endpoints** of the domain. (7) Answer the actual question with units.
@p **Why endpoints matter.** On a closed interval the greatest value can be at an endpoint rather than at a stationary point. Always compare the stationary value with the endpoint values when the domain is closed, e.g. $0\le x\le10$.
@p **Answer the question asked.** Often you find $x$, but the question wants the area, the cost, or the dimensions. Re-read the question after solving.
@ex Fencing against a wall (full working)
Q: A farmer has 120 m of fencing to build a rectangular pen against a straight wall (no fence on the wall side). Find the dimensions that maximise the area.
S: **Define variables:** let $x$ m be each side perpendicular to the wall; the side parallel to the wall is then $120-2x$ m.
S: **Area function:** $A=x(120-2x)=120x-2x^2$.
S: **Domain:** lengths must be positive: $x>0$ and $120-2x>0$, so $0<x<60$.
S: **Differentiate and solve:** $A'=120-4x=0\Rightarrow x=30$.
S: **Justify a maximum:** $A''=-4<0$, so concave down: a maximum.
S: **Answer the question:** the pen is $30$ m by $60$ m (the 60 m side along the wall), with area $30\times60=1800$ m$^2$.
A: $30$ m $\times$ $60$ m, area $1800$ m²
@ex The cheapest can (full working)
Q: A closed cylindrical can must hold $500$ cm$^3$. Find the radius and height that use the least metal.
S: **Variables:** radius $r$ cm, height $h$ cm. Volume: $\pi r^2h=500$, so $h=\dfrac{500}{\pi r^2}$.
S: **Quantity to minimise (surface area):** $S=2\pi r^2+2\pi rh$. Substitute $h$: $S=2\pi r^2+2\pi r\cdot\dfrac{500}{\pi r^2}=2\pi r^2+\dfrac{1000}{r}$, with $r>0$.
S: **Differentiate:** $S'=4\pi r-\dfrac{1000}{r^2}$.
S: **Solve $S'=0$:** $4\pi r=\dfrac{1000}{r^2}\Rightarrow r^3=\dfrac{250}{\pi}\Rightarrow r=\sqrt[3]{\dfrac{250}{\pi}}\approx4.30$ cm.
S: **Justify a minimum:** $S''=4\pi+\dfrac{2000}{r^3}>0$ for all $r>0$.
S: **Height:** $h=\dfrac{500}{\pi(4.30)^2}\approx8.60$ cm (equal to the diameter).
A: $r\approx4.30$ cm, $h\approx8.60$ cm
@ex Profit on a closed interval (full working)
Q: Weekly profit from selling $x$ hundred items is $P=-2x^3+27x^2-84x+20$ thousand dollars, $0\le x\le10$. Find the maximum profit.
S: **Differentiate:** $P'=-6x^2+54x-84=-6(x^2-9x+14)=-6(x-2)(x-7)$.
S: **Stationary points:** $x=2$ and $x=7$.
S: **Nature:** $P''=-12x+54$. $P''(2)=30>0$ (minimum); $P''(7)=-30<0$ (maximum).
S: **Value at the maximum:** $P(7)=-686+1323-588+20=69$.
S: **Check endpoints:** $P(0)=20$ and $P(10)=-2000+2700-840+20=-120$. Both are less than 69.
S: **Answer:** selling 700 items gives the maximum profit of \$69 000.
A: Maximum profit \$69 000 at 700 items
@ex Open box from a sheet (full working)
Q: Squares of side $x$ cm are cut from the corners of a $30\times30$ cm sheet and the sides folded up to make an open box. Find the maximum volume.
S: **Dimensions:** base $(30-2x)$ by $(30-2x)$, height $x$. **Volume:** $V=x(30-2x)^2$, domain $0<x<15$.
S: **Differentiate (product + chain):** $V'=(30-2x)^2+x\cdot2(30-2x)(-2)=(30-2x)\left[(30-2x)-4x\right]=(30-2x)(30-6x)$.
S: **Solve $V'=0$:** $x=15$ (outside the domain) or $x=5$.
S: **Justify:** for $x$ slightly less than 5, $V'>0$; slightly more, $V'<0$: a maximum.
S: **Maximum volume:** $V(5)=5\times20^2=2000$ cm$^3$.
A: $2000$ cm³ when $x=5$ cm
@warn Forgetting to justify the maximum or minimum loses a mark every time. So does giving $x$ when the question asked for the maximum area.
@p **Exam tip.** Begin with a labelled diagram and a sentence defining every variable. Markers award marks for a correct model (the function in one variable with its domain) even if later algebra goes wrong.

## X
@p **Endpoints and justification.** On a closed interval, list endpoints, stationary points and any points where the function is not differentiable, and compare values. Always state which test proves max/min.
@p **Describing graphs in words.** Use full sentences with evidence: "$f''(x)>0$ for $x>1$, so the graph is concave up there". Mixing up $f'$ (increasing/decreasing) with $f''$ (concavity) is a classic lost mark.
@ex Exam-style: the closest point (full working)
Q: Find the point on the curve $y=\sqrt x$ that is closest to $(4,0)$.
S: **Distance squared:** a point on the curve is $(x,\sqrt x)$. Distance squared to $(4,0)$ is $D=(x-4)^2+(\sqrt x)^2=(x-4)^2+x$. Minimising $D$ also minimises the distance (the square root is increasing), and $D$ is easier to differentiate.
S: **Differentiate:** $D'=2(x-4)+1=2x-7$.
S: **Solve:** $x=\frac72$.
S: **Justify:** $D''=2>0$, a minimum.
S: **Point:** $y=\sqrt{3.5}=\frac{\sqrt{14}}{2}\approx1.87$, so $\left(\frac72,\frac{\sqrt{14}}2\right)$. Minimum distance $\sqrt{D}=\sqrt{\frac14+\frac72}=\frac{\sqrt{15}}{2}\approx1.94$.
A: $\left(\frac72,\ \frac{\sqrt{14}}{2}\right)$, distance $\frac{\sqrt{15}}{2}$
@ex Exam-style: inflection of a bell curve (full working)
Q: For $f(x)=e^{-x^2}$, find the exact positive $x$-value of the point of inflection.
S: **First derivative (chain rule):** $f'(x)=-2xe^{-x^2}$.
S: **Second derivative (product rule):** $u=-2x$, $u'=-2$; $v=e^{-x^2}$, $v'=-2xe^{-x^2}$. So $f''=-2e^{-x^2}+(-2x)(-2x)e^{-x^2}=e^{-x^2}(4x^2-2)$.
S: **Set to zero:** $4x^2-2=0\Rightarrow x^2=\frac12\Rightarrow x=\pm\frac1{\sqrt2}$.
S: **Sign change:** $4x^2-2$ changes sign at $x=\frac1{\sqrt2}$, so it is an inflection.
A: $x=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt2}=\dfrac{\sqrt2}{2}$
