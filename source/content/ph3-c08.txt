## 8.1 | The ideal transformer
@p A transformer has a **primary** and **secondary** coil wound on a soft-iron core. An alternating current in the primary produces a changing flux in the core, which induces an emf in the secondary. **It works only with AC**, because a DC current produces a steady flux and no induction.
@key $$\frac{V_s}{V_p}=\frac{N_s}{N_p},\qquad V_pI_p=V_sI_s\ (\text{ideal})$$
@ex Step-down
Q: A transformer has $500$ primary and $100$ secondary turns with a $240\ \text{V}$ input. Find the output voltage.
S: $V_s=V_p\frac{N_s}{N_p}=240\times\frac{100}{500}=48\ \text{V}$.
A: $48\ \text{V}$
@p $N_s>N_p$ is a **step-up** transformer (raises voltage, lowers current). $N_s<N_p$ is **step-down**. The power is not increased: voltage up means current down.
@ex Currents
Q: An ideal transformer delivers $12\ \text{W}$ at $48\ \text{V}$ from a $240\ \text{V}$ primary. Find the secondary and primary currents.
S: $I_s=\frac PV=\frac{12}{48}=0.25\ \text{A}$.
S: $I_p=\frac{12}{240}=0.050\ \text{A}$.
A: $0.25\ \text{A}$ and $0.050\ \text{A}$.
@int transformer | Change the turns and the load. Check that the ideal power in equals the power out.

## 8.2 | Real transformers and energy losses
@p Real transformers are not perfectly efficient. **Efficiency** $=\dfrac{P_{\text{out}}}{P_{\text{in}}}\times100\%$. Losses include **resistive heating** in the windings, **eddy currents** in the core (reduced by using a **laminated** core), **hysteresis** (reversing magnetisation of the core, reduced by soft iron) and **flux leakage** (reduced by winding the coils on a shared core).
@ex Efficiency
Q: A transformer takes $300\ \text{W}$ and delivers $285\ \text{W}$. Find its efficiency.
S: $\frac{285}{300}\times100=95\%$.
A: $95\%$

## 8.3 | Power transmission
@p The power lost in a cable is $P_{\text{loss}}=I^2R$. For a given transmitted power $P=VI$, a **higher voltage means a lower current**, and the loss falls with the **square** of the current. That is why electricity is **stepped up** at the power station for transmission and **stepped down** near the user.
@ex Transmission loss
Q: A power station sends $100\ \text{kW}$ through cables with resistance $5.0\ \Omega$. Compare the power lost at $10\ \text{kV}$ and at $1.0\ \text{kV}$.
S: At $10\ \text{kV}$: $I=\frac{100\times10^3}{10\times10^3}=10\ \text{A}$; $P_{\text{loss}}=10^2(5.0)=500\ \text{W}$ (0.5%).
S: At $1.0\ \text{kV}$: $I=100\ \text{A}$; $P_{\text{loss}}=100^2(5.0)=50\ \text{kW}$ (50%).
A: $500\ \text{W}$ at $10\ \text{kV}$ versus $50\ \text{kW}$ at $1.0\ \text{kV}$.
@warn The voltage in $P_{\text{loss}}=V^2/R$ is the voltage **across the cable**, not the transmission voltage. Use $I^2R$ for line loss.

## X | Exam toolkit
@p **Explain, do not just calculate.** A typical question: "Explain why power is transmitted at high voltage." Answer: for a fixed power, higher voltage means lower current ($I=P/V$), and heat loss in the cables is $I^2R$, so far less energy is wasted.
@p **Reasons a transformer is not 100% efficient** should name the cause and the fix: eddy currents (laminated core), resistive heating (thick copper wire), hysteresis (soft iron core) and leakage (shared core).
