## 7.2
@p **What this topic is about.** A Bernoulli trial is the simplest random experiment: one trial, two outcomes (success or failure). It is the building block of the binomial distribution, which counts successes over many repeated trials.
@key A **Bernoulli random variable** $X$ takes the value 1 (success) with probability $p$ and 0 (failure) with probability $1-p$. $$E(X)=p\qquad\operatorname{Var}(X)=p(1-p)\qquad\sigma=\sqrt{p(1-p)}$$
@p **Where the formulas come from.** $E(X)=1\cdot p+0\cdot(1-p)=p$. $E(X^2)=1^2\cdot p+0^2\cdot(1-p)=p$, so $\operatorname{Var}(X)=p-p^2=p(1-p)$. The variance is largest at $p=0.5$ (maximum uncertainty, $0.25$) and zero at $p=0$ or $1$ (no uncertainty).
@p **Defining success.** "Success" just means the outcome you are counting; it need not be a good thing. If you are counting faulty items, "success" is "faulty". Always state $p$ in words: "$p$ = probability a randomly chosen item is faulty $=0.03$".
@ex Bernoulli mean and SD (full working)
Q: A basketballer makes 70% of free throws. Let $X=1$ if a shot succeeds and $0$ otherwise. Find $E(X)$, $\operatorname{Var}(X)$ and $\sigma$.
S: **Identify $p$:** success = shot made, $p=0.7$.
S: **Mean:** $E(X)=p=0.7$.
S: **Variance:** $\operatorname{Var}(X)=p(1-p)=0.7\times0.3=0.21$.
S: **SD:** $\sigma=\sqrt{0.21}\approx0.458$.
A: $E=0.7$, $\operatorname{Var}=0.21$, $\sigma\approx0.458$
@ex Finding $p$ from the variance (full working)
Q: A Bernoulli variable has variance 0.16 and $p>0.5$. Find $p$.
S: **Set up:** $p(1-p)=0.16\Rightarrow p-p^2=0.16$.
S: **Rearrange:** $p^2-p+0.16=0$.
S: **Solve:** $p=\dfrac{1\pm\sqrt{1-0.64}}{2}=\dfrac{1\pm0.6}{2}$, so $p=0.8$ or $0.2$.
S: **Choose:** $p>0.5$, so $p=0.8$.
A: $p=0.8$
@ex Comparing spread (full working)
Q: Which Bernoulli variable has the larger SD: $p=0.1$ or $p=0.4$?
S: **$p=0.1$:** $\operatorname{Var}=0.1\times0.9=0.09$, $\sigma=0.3$.
S: **$p=0.4$:** $\operatorname{Var}=0.4\times0.6=0.24$, $\sigma\approx0.490$.
S: **Compare:** $0.490>0.3$.
S: **Reason:** $p=0.4$ is closer to 0.5, where uncertainty is greatest.
A: $p=0.4$ (SD $\approx0.49$ versus $0.3$)

## 7.3
@p **What this topic is about.** The **binomial distribution** counts the number of successes in a fixed number of independent, identical Bernoulli trials. It models guessing on tests, defective items in a batch, free throws made, and survey responses. Expect it in both exam papers.
@key $X\sim\text{Bin}(n,p)$ counts successes in $n$ trials. $$P(X=k)=\binom nk p^k(1-p)^{n-k},\quad k=0,1,\dots,n,\qquad\binom nk=\frac{n!}{k!(n-k)!}$$ **Conditions:** fixed $n$; each trial has two outcomes; constant $p$; trials independent.
@p **Why the formula works.** Take $n=3$, $k=2$. The ways to get 2 successes are SSF, SFS, FSS: there are $\binom32=3$ arrangements. Each has probability $p^2(1-p)$ (multiply because trials are independent). So $P(X=2)=3p^2(1-p)$. In general $\binom nk$ counts the arrangements and $p^k(1-p)^{n-k}$ is the probability of each one.
@p **Translating words to inequalities.** "Exactly 3": $X=3$. "At most 3": $X\le3$. "At least 3": $X\ge3=1-P(X\le2)$. "Fewer than 3": $X\le2$. "More than 3": $X\ge4$. "Between 2 and 5 inclusive": $2\le X\le5$. Write the inequality first, then calculate (by formula or CAS `binomCdf`).
@ex Exactly $k$ by formula (full working)
Q: $X\sim\text{Bin}(5,0.3)$. Find $P(X=2)$ to 4 decimal places.
S: **Identify:** $n=5$, $p=0.3$, $k=2$, $1-p=0.7$.
S: **Combination:** $\binom52=\frac{5!}{2!\,3!}=10$.
S: **Formula:** $P(X=2)=10\times0.3^2\times0.7^3$.
S: **Evaluate:** $0.3^2=0.09$, $0.7^3=0.343$, so $10\times0.09\times0.343=0.3087$.
A: $0.3087$
@ex At least one (full working)
Q: A seed germinates with probability 0.8. Ten seeds are planted. Find the probability that at least one fails to germinate.
S: **Define:** let $X$ = number that **fail**; $X\sim\text{Bin}(10,0.2)$.
S: **Translate:** "at least one fails" is $P(X\ge1)$.
S: **Complement:** $P(X\ge1)=1-P(X=0)$.
S: **$P(X=0)$:** $\binom{10}{0}0.2^0\,0.8^{10}=0.8^{10}\approx0.1074$.
S: **Answer:** $1-0.1074=0.8926$.
A: $\approx0.893$
@ex Worded problem: guessing (full working)
Q: A test has 10 multiple-choice questions with 4 options each. A student guesses every answer. Find the probability of at least 5 correct.
S: **Check conditions:** fixed $n=10$; correct/incorrect; $p=0.25$ for each; guesses independent ✓. $X\sim\text{Bin}(10,0.25)$.

## 7.4
@p **What this topic is about.** The mean and spread of a binomial distribution, and how its shape changes with $n$ and $p$. These let you predict typical outcomes, judge whether a result is unusual, and work backwards to find $n$ or $p$.
@key $X\sim\text{Bin}(n,p)$: $$E(X)=np\qquad\operatorname{Var}(X)=np(1-p)\qquad\sigma=\sqrt{np(1-p)}$$
@p **Why.** A binomial count is the sum of $n$ independent Bernoulli variables, each with mean $p$ and variance $p(1-p)$. Means add, and for independent variables variances add, giving $np$ and $np(1-p)$.
@p **Shape.** If $p=0.5$ the distribution is symmetric. If $p<0.5$ it is skewed right (positive skew); if $p>0.5$ skewed left. The most likely value (mode) is near the mean $np$. As $n$ grows the shape becomes more symmetric and bell-like.
@p **Unusual results.** About 95% of outcomes fall within two standard deviations of the mean. A result more than about $2\sigma$ from $np$ is unusual and may suggest the assumed $p$ is wrong.
@ex Mean and SD (full working)
Q: $X\sim\text{Bin}(40,0.15)$. Find $E(X)$, $\operatorname{Var}(X)$ and $\sigma$.
S: **Mean:** $np=40\times0.15=6$.
S: **Variance:** $np(1-p)=6\times0.85=5.1$.
S: **SD:** $\sqrt{5.1}\approx2.26$.
S: **Interpret:** in 40 trials we expect about 6 successes, typically within about 2 of that.
A: $E=6$, $\operatorname{Var}=5.1$, $\sigma\approx2.26$
@ex Finding $n$ and $p$ (full working)
Q: A binomial variable has mean 6 and variance 4.2. Find $n$ and $p$.
S: **Equations:** $np=6$ and $np(1-p)=4.2$.
S: **Divide the second by the first:** $1-p=\frac{4.2}{6}=0.7$, so $p=0.3$.
S: **Find $n$:** $n=\frac{6}{0.3}=20$.
S: **Check:** $20\times0.3\times0.7=4.2$ ✓.
A: $n=20$, $p=0.3$
@ex Worded problem: is the result unusual? (full working)
Q: 4% of components are defective. In a box of 200, 15 are defective. Is this unusual?
S: **Model:** $X\sim\text{Bin}(200,0.04)$.
S: **Mean:** $200\times0.04=8$.
S: **SD:** $\sqrt{200\times0.04\times0.96}=\sqrt{7.68}\approx2.77$.
S: **Standardise:** $\frac{15-8}{2.77}\approx2.5$ standard deviations above the mean.
S: **Conclude:** more than 2 SD above the mean, so unusual; the defect rate may have increased.
A: Yes, about 2.5 SD above the mean of 8
@ex Most likely value (full working)
Q: For $X\sim\text{Bin}(10,0.3)$, which value is most likely?
S: **Estimate from the mean:** $np=3$.
S: **Compare neighbours:** $P(X=2)=\binom{10}2(0.3)^2(0.7)^8\approx0.2335$; $P(X=3)=\binom{10}3(0.3)^3(0.7)^7\approx0.2668$; $P(X=4)\approx0.2001$.

## 7.5
@p **What this topic is about.** Applying binomial models to real situations and judging whether the model is appropriate. Exam questions often finish with "comment on the validity of the binomial model", so you must check each condition in context.
@p **Modelling routine.** (1) Define $X$ in words. (2) Identify $n$ and $p$. (3) Check the four conditions in context. (4) Translate the question into an inequality and calculate. (5) Interpret in context. (6) Comment on assumptions, especially independence and constant $p$.
@p **When the model breaks.** Sampling **without replacement** from a small group changes $p$ after each draw (not constant, not independent). People in the same household or class influence each other (not independent). Weather or seasonal effects change $p$ over time. If the population is large compared with the sample (e.g. 10 from 10 000), sampling without replacement is approximately binomial.
@ex Worded problem: free throws (full working)
Q: A player makes 75% of free throws. In a game she takes 8. Find the probability she makes at least 6, and the expected number made.
S: **Model:** $X$ = number made, $X\sim\text{Bin}(8,0.75)$, assuming shots are independent with constant success rate.
S: **Translate:** $P(X\ge6)=P(X=6)+P(X=7)+P(X=8)$.
S: **Calculate:** $P(X=6)=\binom86(0.75)^6(0.25)^2\approx0.3115$; $P(X=7)=8(0.75)^7(0.25)\approx0.2670$; $P(X=8)=0.75^8\approx0.1001$.
S: **Add:** $\approx0.6785$.
S: **Expected number:** $8\times0.75=6$.
A: $P(X\ge6)\approx0.679$; expected 6 made
@ex Checking the model (full working)
Q: A quality inspector removes 5 bulbs one at a time without replacement from a box of 20, of which 4 are faulty. Is the number of faulty bulbs chosen binomial?
S: **Fixed $n$:** yes, 5 draws.
S: **Two outcomes:** yes, faulty or not.
S: **Constant $p$:** no. The first draw has $p=\frac4{20}$; after a faulty draw the next has $\frac3{19}$; after a good draw it is $\frac4{19}$.
S: **Independence:** no, each draw depends on earlier draws.
A: Not binomial: without replacement from a small box, $p$ changes and trials are dependent
@ex Worded problem: survey (full working)
Q: 30% of a large town's residents support a proposal. 12 residents are surveyed at random. Find the probability that more than 5 support it.
S: **Model:** $X\sim\text{Bin}(12,0.3)$ (the town is large, so sampling is approximately independent with constant $p$).
S: **Translate:** "more than 5" is $X\ge6$, so $P(X\ge6)=1-P(X\le5)$.
S: **CAS:** $P(X\le5)\approx0.8822$.
S: **Answer:** $1-0.8822=0.1178$.
A: $\approx0.118$
