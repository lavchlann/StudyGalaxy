## 2.2 | The unit circle and radians
@p One **radian** is the angle at the centre of a circle subtended by an arc equal in length to the radius. A full turn is $2\pi$ radians, so $\pi$ rad $=180^\circ$. To convert: degrees $\times\frac{\pi}{180}$ gives radians, and radians $\times\frac{180}{\pi}$ gives degrees.
@p On the **unit circle** (radius 1), the point at angle $\theta$ is $(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)$. So $\cos$ is the $x$-coordinate, $\sin$ is the $y$-coordinate, and $\tan\theta=\frac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta}$ is the gradient of the radius.
@key Exact values: $\sin\frac\pi6=\tfrac12$, $\cos\frac\pi6=\tfrac{\sqrt3}2$; $\sin\frac\pi4=\cos\frac\pi4=\tfrac{\sqrt2}2$; $\sin\frac\pi3=\tfrac{\sqrt3}2$, $\cos\frac\pi3=\tfrac12$. Always: $\sin^2\theta+\cos^2\theta=1$.
@p Use the quadrant to find the sign. In quadrant 1 all are positive; 2 only $\sin$; 3 only $\tan$; 4 only $\cos$. Reduce an angle to its **reference angle** in the first quadrant, then apply the sign.
@ex An exact value outside the first quadrant
Q: Find the exact value of $\sin\frac{5\pi}{6}$ and $\cos\frac{7\pi}{6}$.
S: $\frac{5\pi}{6}$ is in quadrant 2 with reference angle $\frac\pi6$; $\sin$ is positive there, so $\sin\frac{5\pi}6=\frac12$.
S: $\frac{7\pi}{6}$ is in quadrant 3 with reference angle $\frac\pi6$; $\cos$ is negative there, so $\cos\frac{7\pi}6=-\frac{\sqrt3}2$.
A: $\tfrac12$ and $-\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}$
@ex Using the Pythagorean identity
Q: If $\sin\theta=\tfrac35$ and $\theta$ is in quadrant 2, find $\cos\theta$ and $\tan\theta$.
S: $\cos^2\theta=1-\tfrac9{25}=\tfrac{16}{25}$, so $\cos\theta=\pm\tfrac45$.
S: Quadrant 2 has negative $\cos$, so $\cos\theta=-\tfrac45$.
S: $\tan\theta=\frac{3/5}{-4/5}=-\tfrac34$.
A: $\cos\theta=-\frac45$, $\tan\theta=-\frac34$
@warn Calculator in the wrong mode is the most common lost mark. If the question uses $\pi$, your CAS or calculator must be in **radians**.

## 2.3 | Trigonometric functions and their graphs
@p For $y=a\sin\big(b(x-c)\big)+d$ (and likewise cosine): **amplitude** $|a|$; **period** $\frac{2\pi}{b}$; **horizontal shift** $c$; **vertical shift** $d$, with the **midline** $y=d$. The maximum is $d+|a|$ and the minimum is $d-|a|$.
@p The graph of $y=\tan x$ has period $\pi$ and vertical asymptotes at $x=\frac\pi2+n\pi$. It has no amplitude.
@ex Reading a graph
Q: State the amplitude, period, and range of $y=3\sin(2x)+1$.
S: Amplitude $=3$. Period $=\frac{2\pi}{2}=\pi$.
S: Midline $y=1$, so the range is $1-3\le y\le 1+3$.
A: Amplitude 3, period $\pi$, range $[-2,4]$.
@p **Solving trig equations** on a given domain: find the reference angle from the inverse trig function, then use symmetry for every solution in the domain. For $\sin$ the two solutions are $\theta$ and $\pi-\theta$; for $\cos$ they are $\theta$ and $2\pi-\theta$; for $\tan$ add $\pi$. If the argument is $2x$, the domain for $2x$ is twice as long, so list more solutions before dividing.
@ex Solving on a domain
Q: Solve $2\sin(2x)=\sqrt3$ for $0\le x\le\pi$.
S: $\sin(2x)=\frac{\sqrt3}2$, and $0\le 2x\le 2\pi$.
S: $2x=\frac\pi3$ or $\frac{2\pi}3$.
S: $x=\frac\pi6$ or $\frac\pi3$.
A: $x=\frac\pi6,\ \frac\pi3$
@warn Change the domain **first**. For $\sin(2x)$ on $0\le x\le\pi$ you need $0\le 2x\le 2\pi$. Forgetting that doubling the argument doubles the interval loses half the solutions.

## 2.4 | Differentiating trigonometric functions
@key $$\frac{d}{dx}\sin x=\cos x,\qquad\frac{d}{dx}\cos x=-\sin x,\qquad\frac{d}{dx}\tan x=\sec^2x=\frac1{\cos^2x}$$
@p With the chain rule: $\frac{d}{dx}\sin(f(x))=f'(x)\cos(f(x))$ and $\frac{d}{dx}\cos(f(x))=-f'(x)\sin(f(x))$. These only hold when $x$ is in **radians**.
@ex Chain rule with trig
Q: Differentiate $y=\sin(3x)+\cos(x^2)$.
S: $\frac{d}{dx}\sin(3x)=3\cos(3x)$.
S: $\frac{d}{dx}\cos(x^2)=-2x\sin(x^2)$.
A: $y'=3\cos(3x)-2x\sin(x^2)$
@ex Stationary points of a trig graph
Q: Find where $y=\sin x+\cos x$ has stationary points on $0\le x\le 2\pi$.
S: $y'=\cos x-\sin x=0\Rightarrow\tan x=1$.
S: $x=\frac\pi4$ or $\frac{5\pi}{4}$.
A: $x=\frac\pi4$ and $x=\frac{5\pi}4$
@warn $\frac{d}{dx}\sin(x^2)\neq\cos(x^2)$. You must multiply by the derivative of the inside. Also, $\frac{d}{dx}\cos x$ is **negative** sine.

## 2.5 | Modelling with trigonometric functions
@p A periodic situation like tides, daylight hours or a Ferris wheel is modelled by $y=a\sin\big(b(t-c)\big)+d$ or a cosine. Steps: find the midline from $\frac{\max+\min}2$; the amplitude from $\frac{\max-\min}2$; the period from the time between peaks; then use $b=\frac{2\pi}{\text{period}}$.
@ex Building a model
Q: Water depth varies between 2 m and 6 m with a period of 12 hours, and is at its maximum at $t=0$. Find a model.
S: Midline $=\frac{2+6}2=4$; amplitude $=2$; $b=\frac{2\pi}{12}=\frac\pi6$.
S: Max at $t=0$ suggests cosine with no shift.
A: $D=2\cos\left(\frac{\pi t}6\right)+4$
@ex Using the model
Q: When is the depth first equal to 5 m?
S: $2\cos\left(\frac{\pi t}{6}\right)+4=5\Rightarrow\cos\left(\frac{\pi t}6\right)=\frac12$.
S: $\frac{\pi t}{6}=\frac\pi3\Rightarrow t=2$.
A: $t=2$ hours
@p Rate of change is the derivative. $\frac{dD}{dt}=-\frac\pi3\sin\left(\frac{\pi t}6\right)$, which is zero at the highest and lowest tide and most negative halfway down.
