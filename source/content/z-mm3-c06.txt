## 6.2
@p **What this topic is about.** A random variable turns the outcome of a chance process into a number, so we can calculate with it. This topic covers **discrete** random variables (which take separate, countable values), how to build and check a probability distribution, and how to calculate probabilities, including cumulative and conditional ones.
@key A **discrete random variable** $X$ takes countable values $x_1,x_2,\dots$ with probabilities $P(X=x)$. A **probability distribution** is valid when (1) $0\le P(X=x)\le1$ for every $x$, and (2) $\sum P(X=x)=1$.
@p **Notation.** Capital $X$ is the random variable (the rule); lowercase $x$ is a particular value it can take. $P(X=2)$ means "the probability that $X$ equals 2". A distribution can be given as a table, a rule (such as $P(X=x)=\frac{x}{10}$), or a graph (a column graph of probabilities).
@p **Building a distribution.** (1) List every possible value of $X$. (2) Find the probability of each, using equally likely outcomes, a tree diagram or a table of outcomes. (3) Check the probabilities add to 1. For two dice, a $6\times6$ grid of the 36 equally likely outcomes is the quickest way to get the distribution of the sum, difference or maximum.
@p **Cumulative probabilities and complements.** $P(X\le k)$ adds the probabilities of every value up to $k$. "At least 3" is $P(X\ge3)=1-P(X\le2)$; "more than 3" is $P(X>3)=P(X\ge4)$; "fewer than 3" is $P(X<3)=P(X\le2)$. Translate the words into an inequality before adding.
@key **Conditional probability:** $P(A\mid B)=\dfrac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)}$. With random variables, e.g. $P(X\ge2\mid X\ge1)=\dfrac{P(X\ge2)}{P(X\ge1)}$, because "$X\ge2$ and $X\ge1$" is just "$X\ge2$".
@ex Finding an unknown constant (full working)
Q: $X$ has $P(X=x)=kx$ for $x=1,2,3,4$. Find $k$, then $P(X\ge3)$ and $P(X<3)$.
S: **List the probabilities in terms of $k$:** $P(X=1)=k$, $P(X=2)=2k$, $P(X=3)=3k$, $P(X=4)=4k$.
S: **Use the sum-to-1 condition:** $k+2k+3k+4k=1\Rightarrow10k=1\Rightarrow k=\frac1{10}$.
S: **Check validity:** the probabilities are $0.1,0.2,0.3,0.4$, all between 0 and 1. ✓
S: **$P(X\ge3)$:** $P(X=3)+P(X=4)=0.3+0.4=0.7$.
S: **$P(X<3)$:** $P(X=1)+P(X=2)=0.1+0.2=0.3$ (or $1-0.7$).
A: $k=\frac1{10}$; $P(X\ge3)=0.7$; $P(X<3)=0.3$
@ex Distribution from two dice (full working)
Q: Two fair dice are rolled and $X$ is the larger of the two numbers. Find the distribution of $X$ and $P(X\le3)$.
S: **Cumulative idea:** $X\le k$ means both dice show at most $k$. Each die does this with probability $\frac k6$, so $P(X\le k)=\left(\frac k6\right)^2=\frac{k^2}{36}$.
S: **Individual probabilities:** $P(X=k)=P(X\le k)-P(X\le k-1)=\frac{k^2-(k-1)^2}{36}=\frac{2k-1}{36}$.
S: **List them:** $x=1,2,3,4,5,6$ with probabilities $\frac1{36},\frac3{36},\frac5{36},\frac7{36},\frac9{36},\frac{11}{36}$.
S: **Check the sum:** $\frac{1+3+5+7+9+11}{36}=\frac{36}{36}=1$ ✓.
S: **$P(X\le3)$:** $\frac{1+3+5}{36}=\frac9{36}=\frac14$.
A: $P(X=k)=\frac{2k-1}{36}$; $P(X\le3)=\frac14$
@ex Conditional probability (full working)
Q: $X$ takes values $0,1,2,3$ with probabilities $0.1,0.3,0.4,0.2$. Find $P(X\ge2\mid X\ge1)$.
S: **Formula:** $P(X\ge2\mid X\ge1)=\dfrac{P(X\ge2\text{ and }X\ge1)}{P(X\ge1)}$.
S: **Simplify the intersection:** if $X\ge2$ then automatically $X\ge1$, so the top is $P(X\ge2)=0.4+0.2=0.6$.
S: **Bottom:** $P(X\ge1)=1-P(X=0)=0.9$.
S: **Divide:** $\dfrac{0.6}{0.9}=\dfrac23$.
A: $\frac23$
@ex Checking validity (full working)
Q: Is $P(X=x)=\frac{x^2}{30}$ for $x=1,2,3,4$ a valid probability distribution?
S: **Compute each probability:** $\frac1{30},\frac4{30},\frac9{30},\frac{16}{30}$.
S: **Condition 1:** each is between 0 and 1 ✓.
S: **Condition 2:** $\frac{1+4+9+16}{30}=\frac{30}{30}=1$ ✓.
S: **Conclude:** both conditions hold, so it is valid.
A: Yes, it is valid

## 6.3
@p **What this topic is about.** The **expected value** (mean) of a random variable is its long-run average. It is used to compare options, set fair prices for games and insurance, and summarise a distribution with one number.
@key $$E(X)=\mu=\sum x\,P(X=x)\qquad E(aX+b)=aE(X)+b\qquad E\big(g(X)\big)=\sum g(x)P(X=x)$$
@p **Meaning.** Multiply each value by its probability and add. If you repeated the experiment many times and averaged the results, you would get close to $E(X)$. It does not have to be a value $X$ can take: the mean of a fair die is 3.5. Think of it as the balance point of the probability graph.
@p **Linear changes.** If every value is multiplied by $a$ and then $b$ is added (e.g. converting to dollars and adding a fee), the mean changes in the same way: $E(aX+b)=aE(X)+b$. This saves recalculating the whole table.
@p **Fair games and net gain.** In money questions, define $X$ as the **net** gain (winnings minus the cost to play). A game is **fair** when $E(\text{net gain})=0$. If $E<0$ the player loses on average (the house profits).
@ex Expected value from a table (full working)
Q: $X$ takes values $0,1,2$ with probabilities $0.2,0.5,0.3$. Find $E(X)$ and $E(3X+1)$.
S: **Multiply each value by its probability:** $0\times0.2=0$, $1\times0.5=0.5$, $2\times0.3=0.6$.
S: **Add:** $E(X)=0+0.5+0.6=1.1$.
S: **Linear rule:** $E(3X+1)=3E(X)+1$.
S: **Substitute:** $3(1.1)+1=4.3$.
A: $E(X)=1.1$; $E(3X+1)=4.3$
@ex Fair game (full working)
Q: You pay \$2 to roll a fair die. You win \$6 for a six and nothing otherwise. Is the game fair? What prize would make it fair?
S: **Define the net gain $G$:** a six gives $6-2=+4$ (probability $\frac16$); otherwise $-2$ (probability $\frac56$).
S: **Expected net gain:** $E(G)=4\times\frac16+(-2)\times\frac56=\frac46-\frac{10}6=-1$.
S: **Interpret:** on average you lose \$1 per game, so it is not fair.
S: **Fair prize $w$:** need $(w-2)\frac16-2\cdot\frac56=0\Rightarrow\frac{w-2}{6}=\frac{10}6\Rightarrow w-2=10\Rightarrow w=12$.
A: Not fair ($E=-\$1$); a \$12 prize makes it fair
@ex Finding unknowns from the mean (full working)
Q: $X$ takes values $1,2,3$ with $P(X=1)=a$, $P(X=2)=b$, $P(X=3)=0.3$, and $E(X)=2.1$. Find $a$ and $b$.
S: **Sum to 1:** $a+b+0.3=1\Rightarrow a+b=0.7$.
S: **Mean equation:** $1a+2b+3(0.3)=2.1\Rightarrow a+2b=1.2$.
S: **Subtract the first equation from the second:** $b=0.5$.
S: **Back-substitute:** $a=0.7-0.5=0.2$.
A: $a=0.2$, $b=0.5$
@ex Worded problem: insurance (full working)
Q: A \$300 policy has a 0.2% chance of a \$50 000 claim, a 1% chance of a \$5000 claim, and otherwise no claim. Find the insurer's expected profit per policy.
S: **Define profit $P$ and list outcomes:** no claim $+300$ (prob $0.988$); small claim $300-5000=-4700$ (prob $0.01$); large claim $300-50000=-49700$ (prob $0.002$).
S: **Check probabilities:** $0.988+0.01+0.002=1$ ✓.
S: **Expected profit:** $300(0.988)-4700(0.01)-49700(0.002)$.
S: **Evaluate:** $296.4-47-99.4=150$.
A: \$150 per policy on average
@warn Remember to subtract the cost to play when finding expected **net** gain, and do not confuse $E(X)$ with the most likely value.
@p **Exam tip.** Present expected-value calculations as a table with a row for $x$, a row for $P(X=x)$, and a row for $xP(X=x)$, then sum the last row.

## 6.4
@p **What this topic is about.** Variance and standard deviation measure the **spread** of a distribution: how far values typically are from the mean. Two options can have the same mean but very different risk, and variance is how we measure that difference.
@key $$\operatorname{Var}(X)=E\big[(X-\mu)^2\big]=E(X^2)-\big[E(X)\big]^2\qquad\sigma=\operatorname{SD}(X)=\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(X)}$$ $$\operatorname{Var}(aX+b)=a^2\operatorname{Var}(X)\qquad\operatorname{SD}(aX+b)=|a|\,\sigma$$
@p **What variance means.** Variance is the average **squared** distance from the mean. Squaring makes every distance positive and weights large deviations more. The standard deviation undoes the squaring so it is in the same units as $X$. A useful rule of thumb: most of the probability lies within about two standard deviations of the mean.
@p **The computational formula.** $E(X^2)=\sum x^2P(X=x)$ (square the values, not the probabilities). Then $\operatorname{Var}(X)=E(X^2)-\mu^2$. This is usually faster than the definition.
@p **Why adding a constant does not change spread.** Adding $b$ shifts every value by the same amount, so distances from the mean are unchanged. Multiplying by $a$ stretches every distance by $|a|$, so the variance (squared distances) is multiplied by $a^2$.
@ex Variance with a table (full working)
Q: $X$ takes values $0,1,2$ with probabilities $0.25,0.5,0.25$. Find $\operatorname{Var}(X)$, $\sigma$ and $\operatorname{Var}(3X-2)$.
S: **Mean:** $\mu=0(0.25)+1(0.5)+2(0.25)=1$.
S: **$E(X^2)$:** square the values: $0^2(0.25)+1^2(0.5)+2^2(0.25)=0+0.5+1=1.5$.
S: **Variance:** $1.5-1^2=0.5$.
S: **Standard deviation:** $\sigma=\sqrt{0.5}\approx0.707$.
S: **Transformed variance:** $\operatorname{Var}(3X-2)=3^2\times0.5=4.5$ (the $-2$ has no effect).
A: $\operatorname{Var}(X)=0.5$, $\sigma\approx0.707$, $\operatorname{Var}(3X-2)=4.5$
@ex Variance of a die (full working)
Q: Find the variance and standard deviation of the score on a fair six-sided die.
S: **Mean:** $\mu=\frac{1+2+3+4+5+6}6=3.5$.
S: **$E(X^2)$:** $\frac{1+4+9+16+25+36}6=\frac{91}6\approx15.17$.
S: **Variance:** $\frac{91}6-3.5^2=\frac{91}6-\frac{49}4=\frac{182-147}{12}=\frac{35}{12}\approx2.92$.
S: **SD:** $\sqrt{35/12}\approx1.71$.
A: $\operatorname{Var}=\frac{35}{12}\approx2.92$, $\sigma\approx1.71$
@ex Using variance rules (full working)
Q: A game's winnings $W$ have $E(W)=3$ and $\operatorname{Var}(W)=4$. A player's profit is $P=5W-8$. Find $E(P)$ and $\sigma(P)$.
S: **Mean:** $E(P)=5E(W)-8=15-8=7$.
S: **Variance:** $\operatorname{Var}(P)=5^2\operatorname{Var}(W)=25\times4=100$.
S: **SD:** $\sigma(P)=\sqrt{100}=10$ (or $|5|\times\sqrt4=10$).
S: **Interpret:** the player gains \$7 per game on average, with a typical spread of \$10.
A: $E(P)=7$, $\sigma(P)=10$
@ex Working backwards (full working)
Q: $X$ has mean 3 and variance 2. Find $E(X^2)$.
S: **Rearrange the formula:** $\operatorname{Var}(X)=E(X^2)-\mu^2\Rightarrow E(X^2)=\operatorname{Var}(X)+\mu^2$.
S: **Substitute:** $E(X^2)=2+3^2$.
S: **Evaluate:** $2+9=11$.
S: **Check:** $11-9=2$ ✓.
A: $E(X^2)=11$
@warn $E(X^2)\neq\big(E(X)\big)^2$; the difference between them is the variance. And $\operatorname{Var}(3X)=9\operatorname{Var}(X)$, not $3\operatorname{Var}(X)$.
@p **Exam tip.** Add an $x^2P(X=x)$ row to your table. Then $E(X)$ and $E(X^2)$ come from the same table, and the variance is one line.

## 6.5
@p **What this topic is about.** Using discrete random variables to make decisions in context: pricing games, comparing investments, insurance, quality control. The mathematics is the mean and SD; the marks come from defining variables clearly, building the right distribution, and interpreting results in context.
@p **Modelling routine.** (1) Define $X$ in words, with units ("$X$ is the profit in dollars from one ticket"). (2) List its values and probabilities in a table; check they sum to 1. (3) Calculate $E(X)$ and, if risk matters, $\sigma$. (4) Interpret: "on average the business makes \$150 per policy". (5) Recommend: choose the option with the better mean, or the lower SD for a cautious decision-maker, and justify with numbers.
@p **Same mean, different risk.** Investment A returning \$100 or \$300 equally likely and investment B returning \$0 or \$400 equally likely both average \$200. But B's outcomes are further from the mean, so its SD is larger and it is riskier. A good answer states both numbers and links the choice to the person's attitude to risk.
@ex Comparing investments (full working)
Q: Investment A returns \$100 or \$300 with equal probability. Investment B returns \$0 or \$400 with equal probability. Compare them.
S: **Means:** $E(A)=\frac{100+300}2=200$; $E(B)=\frac{0+400}2=200$.
S: **Variance of A:** $E(A^2)=\frac{10000+90000}2=50000$, so $\operatorname{Var}(A)=50000-200^2=10000$ and $\sigma_A=100$.
S: **Variance of B:** $E(B^2)=\frac{0+160000}2=80000$, so $\operatorname{Var}(B)=80000-40000=40000$ and $\sigma_B=200$.
S: **Compare:** equal expected returns, but B's spread is double A's.
S: **Recommend:** a cautious investor should choose A (lower risk for the same average return).
A: Both average \$200; A has $\sigma=100$, B has $\sigma=200$; A is less risky
@ex Raffle (full working)
Q: A raffle sells 500 tickets at \$2 each. There is one \$400 prize and two \$50 prizes. Find the expected net gain per ticket.
S: **Define $G$ (net gain):** win \$400: $G=398$, probability $\frac1{500}$; win \$50: $G=48$, probability $\frac2{500}$; no prize: $G=-2$, probability $\frac{497}{500}$.
S: **Check:** $\frac{1+2+497}{500}=1$ ✓.
S: **Expected value:** $398\cdot\frac1{500}+48\cdot\frac2{500}-2\cdot\frac{497}{500}=\frac{398+96-994}{500}$.
S: **Evaluate:** $\frac{-500}{500}=-1$.
A: $-\$1$ per ticket (the organisers raise \$1 per ticket on average)
@ex Quality control (full working)
Q: A machine produces 0, 1 or 2 faulty parts per hour with probabilities 0.7, 0.2, 0.1. Each faulty part costs \$15. Find the expected hourly cost and its standard deviation.
S: **Mean number of faults:** $E(F)=0(0.7)+1(0.2)+2(0.1)=0.4$.
S: **$E(F^2)$:** $0+1(0.2)+4(0.1)=0.6$, so $\operatorname{Var}(F)=0.6-0.16=0.44$.
S: **Cost $C=15F$:** $E(C)=15\times0.4=\$6$ per hour.
S: **SD:** $\operatorname{SD}(C)=15\sqrt{0.44}\approx15\times0.663\approx\$9.95$.
A: Expected cost \$6 per hour, SD about \$9.95
@ex Choosing a price (full working)
Q: A stall's game pays \$10 with probability 0.15, \$2 with probability 0.3, and nothing otherwise. What should it charge so the stall expects to make \$1 per play?
S: **Expected payout:** $10(0.15)+2(0.3)+0(0.55)=1.5+0.6=2.1$.
S: **Stall's profit per play:** price minus payout; expected profit $=c-2.1$.
S: **Set equal to \$1:** $c-2.1=1$.
S: **Solve:** $c=3.1$, i.e. \$3.10 per play.
A: Charge \$3.10
@warn Always define the random variable in words before calculating, and keep track of whether you are working with winnings or net gain.
@p **Exam tip.** End with a recommendation that refers to your numbers: "Option A is better for a cautious investor because it has the same mean (\$200) but half the standard deviation."
